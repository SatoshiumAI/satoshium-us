## Purpose

The Satoshium Suite Governance Review establishes the formal governance model for the completed, operational Satoshium Suite.

Its purpose is to determine how the Suite remains coherent, accountable, reviewable, historically preserved, and capable of governed evolution without collapsing the independent authority of its institutions.

The review does not create a new institutional architecture.

It governs the architecture that already exists.

### Suite Governance Standard

The Suite Governance Standard establishes high-level governance intent around ownership, stewardship, responsibility, change management, version control, transparency, public review, and historical preservation. Its detailed mechanisms remain planned.

### Registry Governance

Registry provides the most mature institution-level governance implementation currently reviewed. It addresses authority, decision rights, approvals, accountability, delegation, separation of duties, decision records, Rules/Policies/Procedures governance, schema and controlled-value governance, identifiers, corrections, lifecycle, exceptions, conflict resolution, reconsideration, emergency governance, transparency, and historical preservation.

Registry is therefore a strong governance precedent, but it is not Suite-wide governance authority.

### Certifier Governance

Certifier provides a narrower stewardship model. It confirms that institution-specific governance remains aligned with Suite Standards and Suite Methodology, preserves operational consistency and version continuity, protects decision integrity, and governs implementation without redefining Suite-wide rules.

### Anchor Governance

The current Anchor Governance page reflects an earlier exploratory governance concept centered on identity, claims, attestations, reputation, and trust systems.

It does not reflect Anchor's reconciled current institutional role as the Suite institution for Integrity Preservation.

Accordingly:

> **The current Anchor Governance page is retained as review evidence but is not treated as governing current-state Suite architecture.**

Its presence is a current-state documentation issue to be carried into the Governance Review correction path rather than used to redefine Anchor or Suite governance.

## Governing Principles

### 1. Governance Preserves Architecture; It Does Not Silently Redesign It

The Governance Review consumes the completed Suite architecture.

Governance may define how that architecture is maintained and changed.

It may not silently redefine the architecture while claiming merely to administer it.

### 2. Suite Governance Protects the Whole; Institutional Governance Protects the Institution

Suite governance addresses matters whose scope is genuinely Suite-wide.

Institution-specific governance remains authoritative for matters within the institution's settled responsibility and canonical ownership.

Suite governance does not absorb routine institutional authority merely because the institution belongs to the Suite.

### 3. Institutional Sovereignty Is Preserved

Each institution retains authority over the matters assigned to it by the reconciled architecture.

Participation in the Suite does not erase institutional boundaries.

Coordination, interoperability, and governance alignment do not create ownership.

### 4. Reference Does Not Transfer Authority

A reference, registration, historical record, integrity reference, discovery signal, workflow, certification artifact, attestation, or Trust Statement does not transfer the originating institution's authority to another institution.

### 5. Delegation Does Not Eliminate Accountability

Authority may be delegated within a governed scope.

The delegating institution remains accountable for the authority it delegates unless the Suite architecture is formally changed through an authorized governance process.

### 6. Material Decisions Must Be Authorized

Material governance decisions must identify competent authority, scope, rationale, effective date, affected materials or institutions, implementation requirements, review conditions, and historical disposition.

### 7. Material Decisions Must Be Documented and Reviewable

Governance decisions must remain reconstructable, preserving what was decided, who had authority, why the decision was made, what changed, when it became effective, and what decision, if any, it superseded.

### 8. Governance Must Be Versioned Without Erasing History

Current governance and historical governance must remain distinguishable.

Supersession does not erase the superseded decision.

Correction does not erase the corrected record.

### 9. Exceptions Do Not Silently Rewrite Rules

A governed exception changes application in a defined case.

It does not silently amend the governing Rule, Policy, Standard, Methodology, or constitutional boundary.

### 10. Operational Convenience Does Not Create Authority

Implementation practice, workflow automation, technical dependency, public-page wording, or historical habit cannot independently create new governance authority.

### 11. Governance Must Preserve Separation of Duties Where Material

Proposal, review, approval, implementation, publication, validation, and historical review may be separated when significance, risk, or reversibility warrants it.

### 12. Transparency and Preservation Are Governance Requirements

Governance must preserve appropriate visibility into current governing materials, effective dates, material decisions, amendments, corrections, exceptions, conflicts, superseded materials, and review history.

## Relationship to the Completed Interoperability Review

The completed Interoperability Review establishes how independently governed institutions may exchange, resolve, serialize, reference, hand off, and preserve governed information while retaining institutional identity and authority.

The Governance Review inherits that boundary.

Interoperability may create interfaces, reference contracts, serialization requirements, compatibility requirements, resolution behavior, handoff mechanics, state/version propagation rules, and exchange constraints.

Interoperability does **not** create hidden governance authority.

Accordingly:

> **Interoperability defines how governed institutions interact. Governance defines how authority and change are controlled. Neither may silently redefine the other.**

Where an interoperability mechanism appears to create de facto authority, dependency, or ownership beyond the settled architecture, that condition must be escalated as a governance issue rather than accepted through implementation practice.
