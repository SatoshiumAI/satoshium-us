## Purpose

This document establishes the final **Satoshium Suite Governance Model**.

It consolidates the governance architecture developed and tested across the full Suite Governance Review and incorporates the clarifications produced by Day 4 adversarial testing.

The model governs:

- Suite-wide constitutional authority;
- institutional sovereignty;
- canonical ownership;
- institutional change;
- Suite membership;
- governance decisions;
- review and approval;
- interoperability boundaries;
- versioning;
- supersession;
- correction;
- transparency;
- historical preservation;
- constitutional evolution.

The governing principle is:

> **Govern the Suite that exists. Preserve institutional sovereignty. Require explicit authority for material change. Preserve history. Never allow implementation, interoperability, reference, documentation, or repeated practice to silently create authority.**

# IV. Institutional Sovereignty

Each institution retains exclusive authority within its settled domain.

Institutional sovereignty includes authority over:

- institution-owned canonical objects;
- local Rules;
- local Policies;
- local Procedures;
- institution-specific lifecycle decisions;
- institution-specific corrections;
- institution-specific versioning;
- institution-specific conclusions and determinations.

But:

```text
EXCLUSIVE INSTITUTIONAL AUTHORITY
≠ UNLIMITED AUTHORITY

SUITE CONSTRAINT
≠ SUITE OWNERSHIP
```

Institutions remain subject to valid Suite-level constitutional constraints, Standards, Methodology, and interoperability requirements where applicable.

# VI. Governance Layers

The Suite Governance Model contains distinct layers with different responsibilities.

## 1. Institutional Governance

Governs institution-owned operations within settled authority.

## 2. Suite Governance

Governs Suite-wide constitutional coherence, shared boundaries, membership, and formal architectural change.

## 3. Suite Standards

Define shared expectations.

## 4. Suite Methodology

Defines shared implementation principles and repeatable methods.

## 5. Interoperability Governance

Defines governed exchange across institutional boundaries.

## 6. Architecture Reconciliation

Resolves contradictions involving settled architecture.

## 7. Governance Decision Records

Preserve individual governance decisions.

## 8. Governance Decision Register

Indexes governance decisions and exposes current standing and historical lineage.

## 9. Versioning / Supersession / Correction Controls

Govern change over time.

## 10. Transparency Controls

Make current authority and status understandable.

## 11. Historical Preservation

Preserves prior governance states.

## 12. Constitutional Change Controls

Protect membership, authority, canonical ownership, role, and institutional structure.

No major layer is redundant.

# VII. Suite Governance vs. Institutional Governance

The governing boundary is:

> **Suite Governance governs the boundaries that make the Suite coherent. Institutional Governance governs the work that makes each institution operational.**

Suite Governance may govern:

- formal Suite membership;
- shared constitutional rules;
- canonical ownership changes;
- institutional-role changes;
- authority redistribution;
- Suite-wide Standards;
- Suite-wide Methodology;
- common governance controls;
- constitutional admission, merger, division, retirement, removal, or succession.

Institutional Governance may govern:

- Routine changes within settled authority;
- local Rules;
- local Policies;
- local Procedures;
- implementation details;
- institution-owned lifecycle and correction;
- local publication;
- supporting structures.

The key distinction is:

```text
CHANGE WITHIN AUTHORITY
→ institution may govern

CHANGE TO AUTHORITY
→ Suite Governance must govern
```

---

# VIII. Change Classification

All governance changes must be classified by their **highest-order actual effect**, not by where they appear.

Change types include:

```text
Documentation Correction
Operational Refinement
Methodology Change
Governance Change
Canonical-Object Change
Institutional-Role Change
Constitutional Change
```

The formal governance classes are:

```text
ROUTINE
→ Institution-Level

MATERIAL
→ Institution-Owned or Suite-Owned,
   with required shared-impact review

CONSTITUTIONAL
→ Formal Suite Governance Decision
```

---

# IX. Routine Change

Routine change:

- stays within settled institutional authority;
- does not alter canonical ownership;
- does not alter institutional role;
- does not alter Suite membership;
- does not alter constitutional dependency;
- does not transfer authority.

Routine decisions may be governed institutionally.

---

# X. Material Change

Material change has meaningful cross-Suite, governance, standards, interoperability, or canonical impact without necessarily altering constitutional allocation.

The clarified rule is:

```text
Material institution-owned change
→ institution retains determination and approval authority
→ Suite reviews shared impact

Material Suite-owned change
→ appropriate Suite authority determines and approves
```

Therefore:

> **Suite review does not automatically create Suite approval authority over an institution-owned Material decision.**

Review is not ownership.

Review is not approval unless the governed subject requires that approval.

---

# XI. Constitutional Change

Constitutional change includes:

- formal Suite membership change;
- canonical ownership transfer;
- institutional-role change;
- authority redistribution;
- institutional merger;
- institutional division;
- institutional retirement;
- institutional removal;
- successor replacement;
- creation of mandatory constitutional dependency;
- creation of a new formal Suite institution.

Constitutional change requires:

```text
Formal Suite Governance
+
Architecture Reconciliation
+
Governance Decision Record
+
Historical Preservation
```

as applicable.

---

# XII. Institutional Change

An institutional change is:

> **A governed change that alters formal identity, settled role, authority boundary, canonical-object ownership, constitutional position, or institutional existence.**

Ordinary evolution changes how an institution performs its settled role.

Institutional change changes the role, authority, ownership, or constitutional status itself.

---

# XIII. New Institution Admission

A future Suite institution must satisfy:

```text
Distinct Institutional Responsibility
Demonstrated Constitutional Need
Non-Duplication
Distinct Canonical Responsibility
Explicit Authority Boundary
Independent Institutional Sovereignty
Interoperability Without Authority Transfer
Formal Constitutional Admission
```

The governing rule is:

```text
NEW CAPABILITY
≠ NEW INSTITUTION
```

Formal Suite membership cannot arise from:

- branding;
- documentation;
- website navigation;
- repository placement;
- public importance;
- operational maturity;
- workflow centrality;
- technical integration;
- repeated use.

There is no de facto Suite membership.

Historical records of the eight-institution era remain preserved.

# XV. Supporting Objects and Canonicalization

A supporting object does not become canonical merely because it gains:

- a stable identifier;
- schema;
- lifecycle;
- versioning;
- publication;
- correction handling;
- external references.

The governing distinction is:

```text
SUPPORTING OBJECT
≠ CANONICAL OBJECT

SCHEMA
≠ CANONICAL OBJECT

TECHNICAL MATURITY
≠ CANONICAL STATUS
```

If a supporting object develops independent identity, lifecycle, versioning, publication, correction authority, and authoritative cross-institution usage:

```text
Explicit Canonical-Status Review
→ REQUIRED
```

Those are canonicalization signals.

They do not themselves create canonical status.

---

# XVI. Functional Ownership Test

Canonical ownership is tested functionally as well as nominally.

Ask:

```text
Who controls canonical identity?
Who controls lifecycle?
Who controls correction?
Who controls versioning?
Who controls publication?
Who controls substantive canonical state?
```

If essential canonical powers materially move to another institution:

```text
Potential Canonical Ownership Transfer
→ Constitutional Review Required
```

An institution cannot remain owner in name while another institution quietly acquires the core ownership powers.

# XX. Review Escalation

Governance review follows impact.

## Institution-Level Review

Required for institution-owned matters.

## Suite-Level Review

Required when shared Suite rules, another institution, or Suite-wide coherence are materially affected.

## Interoperability Review

Required when exchange, references, schemas, serialization, identifiers, compatibility, or propagation are materially affected.

## Architecture Reconciliation

Required when the matter:

- contradicts settled architecture;
- reopens canonical ownership;
- alters institutional role;
- creates unclear authority;
- creates constitutional dependency;
- conflicts with settled Suite semantics.

---

# XXI. Interoperability Governance

The governing relationship is:

```text
SETTLED INSTITUTIONAL ARCHITECTURE
        ↓
INTEROPERABILITY GOVERNANCE
        ↓
EXCHANGE / COMPATIBILITY / HANDOFF
        ↓
IMPLEMENTATION
```

Interoperability may govern:

- reference contracts;
- serialization;
- schema compatibility;
- identifier resolution;
- handoff mechanics;
- version propagation;
- state propagation;
- correction propagation;
- compatibility expectations;
- migration guidance.

Interoperability may not silently govern:

- institutional role;
- canonical ownership;
- Suite membership;
- source authority;
- institutional sovereignty;
- constitutional dependency;
- institutional merger;
- canonical-object identity.

The controlling rule is:

> **Interoperability may constrain exchange, but it may not reallocate authority.**

And:

```text
INTEROPERABILITY FAILURE
≠ SOURCE INVALIDITY
```

---

# XXII. Architecture Reconciliation

Architecture Reconciliation protects settled architecture when:

- governance instruments conflict;
- ownership is ambiguous;
- roles overlap;
- implementation creates de facto authority;
- interoperability pressure implies architectural redesign;
- documentation contradicts settled structure.

Its governing ladder is:

```text
CONFIRM
→ CLARIFY
→ RECONCILE
→ CORRECT
→ CHANGE
```

The rule is:

> **Governance coherence should be restored at the lowest sufficient level of intervention.**

Change is the last resort.

---

# XXIII. Governance Versioning

Governance versioning is:

> **The controlled issuance, identification, supersession, correction, preservation, and historical linking of authoritative governance states over time.**

Core distinctions:

```text
VERSION
≠ DECISION

CORRECTION
≠ VERSION

SUPERSESSION
≠ MUTATION

CHANGED CONCLUSION
=
NEW GOVERNANCE DECISION
```

Version numbering describes editions.

Classification determines review.

---

# XXIV. Supersession

Supersession replaces current operative standing without rewriting history.

```text
Prior Decision
→ historically valid for its effective period

New Decision
→ controlling prospectively

Relationship
→ SUPERSEDES
```

The rule is:

> **Supersession replaces current operative authority without rewriting historical authority.**

Prior and successor decisions must remain linked.

---

# XXV. Governance Correction

Four correction classes are recognized:

```text
Erroneous Governance Text
Incorrect Implementation
Obsolete Governance
Conflicting Governance
```

Their treatment is:

```text
Erroneous text
→ correct to valid governance

Incorrect implementation
→ restore conformity

Obsolete governance
→ supersede / deprecate / archive

Conflicting governance
→ CONFIRM → CLARIFY → RECONCILE → CORRECT → CHANGE
```

Material corrections may not be silently erased.

---

# XXVI. Governance Decision Record

The Governance Decision Record (GDR) is:

> **The authoritative record of a governance determination and its approved operative effect.**

Recommended identifier:

```text
GDR-YYYY-NNNN
```

Every formal GDR must preserve enough information to reconstruct:

- what was decided;
- who had authority;
- why;
- what it affected;
- when it governed;
- what it replaced or corrected.

# XXIX. Transparency

Governance transparency requires:

- current governance must be identifiable;
- authority must be visible;
- decision lineage must be visible;
- Material and Constitutional change must be explainable;
- Proposal must not masquerade as approved governance;
- implementation status must be explicit where material;
- historical records must be clearly distinguishable from current authority.

Transparency is a governance control, not a presentation preference.

# XXX. Historical Preservation

The governing rule is:

```text
CURRENT GOVERNANCE
→ may change

HISTORICAL GOVERNANCE
→ must remain true
```

Therefore:

> **Change prospectively. Preserve retrospectively.**

Superseded governance remains historically valid for the period during which it governed.

Historical architecture must not be rewritten to match later constitutional change.

- current effective governance;

- prior decisions;
