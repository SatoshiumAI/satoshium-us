Suite · Standards

Interoperability Standard

The Interoperability Standard defines shared expectations for cross-institution exchange, compatibility, references, identifiers, serialization, resolution, handoffs, and meaning preservation.

Interoperability operates within settled institutional architecture. It connects institutions without redefining their roles, authority, canonical ownership, or constitutional relationships.

Governing Hierarchy

Settled Institutional Architecture → Suite Governance → Interoperability → Implementation

Interoperability is subordinate to settled institutional architecture. Technical compatibility rules may constrain exchange, but they may not silently redesign institutional authority or constitutional architecture.

Shared Interfaces

Defines common requirements for references, identifiers, schemas, serialization, resolution, handoffs, state/version propagation, and failure handling.

Governance Boundary

INTEROPERABILITY FAILURE ≠ SOURCE INVALIDITY.
COMPATIBILITY REVIEW ≠ GOVERNANCE VETO.
INTEROPERABILITY REVIEW ≠ AUTOMATIC APPROVAL AUTHORITY.
INTEROPERABILITY ≠ AUTHORITY TRANSFER.

A receiving institution may reject an incompatible exchange without invalidating the source institution's canonical object. Compatibility review may identify exchange consequences and implementation requirements, but it does not acquire constitutional or institutional approval authority.

Constitutional Escalation

If interoperability exposes a proposed change to institutional role, canonical ownership, Suite membership, sovereignty, source authority, constitutional dependency, canonical-object identity, or mandatory Suite-wide architecture, the matter must move to Suite Governance / Architecture Reconciliation.

Interoperability may identify a constitutional issue. It may not resolve one by technical convention.

Standard Role

The Interoperability Standard provides the common exchange and compatibility expectations that allow independently governed institutions to work together while preserving distinct canonical objects, provenance, lifecycle, governance, and authority.

Interoperability may constrain exchange, but it may not reallocate authority.
Systems become stronger when their records can speak the same language without surrendering authority.
