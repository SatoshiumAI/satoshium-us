# ATLAS Signal Types v1

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the signal taxonomy used by Atlas jurisdiction packages.

Signals describe structural indicators that influence interpretation posture.

Signals do not establish evidence.

Signals do not assign topology classification.

Signals do not change lifecycle state.

Signals may inform readiness, adjacency continuity, and expansion-edge interpretation.

The purpose of the signal layer is to preserve structured forward visibility without altering the authority boundaries of evidence, topology, or lifecycle governance.

## 2. Signal Authority Boundary

Signals may:
- indicate emerging infrastructure
- indicate pipeline development
- indicate adjacency strengthening
- indicate constraint evolution
- indicate corridor continuity preparation

Signals must not:
- establish corridor attachment
- override interpretation boundary
- replace evidence
- introduce classification authority
- modify lifecycle status

Signals are a governed interpretive layer.

They help explain direction, movement, and conditional emergence.

They do not independently authorize canonical placement, readiness state, or lifecycle promotion.

## 3. Core Signal Categories Overview

The allowed signal classes are:
- pipeline signals
- absence signals
- adjacency signals
- federal overlay signals
- capacity signals
- constraint signals
- transition signals

Each signal must belong to one category.

If a signal appears to touch multiple categories, its dominant interpretive function shall determine its classification.

Signals must remain interpretable within taxonomy boundaries.

Signal categories organize interpretation.

They do not replace the authority hierarchy of `evidence -> signals -> trust -> profile -> builder`.

## 4. Pipeline Signals

Pipeline signals describe:
- planned infrastructure
- announced facilities
- authorized deployments not yet operational
- construction-phase infrastructure
- federally designated but not activated corridor participation

Pipeline signals indicate directional readiness.

They do not establish corridor attachment independently.

A pipeline signal may support future topology interpretation or readiness reasoning, but it remains subordinate to evidence authority.

## 5. Absence Signals

Absence signals describe:
- missing anchor infrastructure
- deployment gaps
- classification limits
- unmet corridor prerequisites
- structural exclusions

Absence signals strengthen interpretation boundary clarity.

They do not imply weakness.

They define topology limits, readiness limits, or interpretive non-establishment where those conditions matter.

Absence signals are valid signals because missing conditions can be structurally meaningful within Atlas interpretation.

## 6. Adjacency Signals

Adjacency signals describe:
- strengthening inter-jurisdiction continuity
- shared infrastructure dependence
- cross-border attachment preparation
- multi-state corridor chaining signals

Adjacency signals may support adjacency interpretation.

They must not independently establish adjacency classification.

Adjacency classification remains evidence-grounded and interpretation-boundary constrained.

Signals may explain strengthening adjacency logic, but they may not create it canonically on their own.

## 7. Federal Overlay Signals

Federal overlay signals describe:
- federal corridor designations
- national infrastructure siting
- research facility alignment
- grid modernization corridors
- defense infrastructure continuity

Federal overlay signals may support classification refinement.

They do not independently establish anchor placement.

Federal overlay signals may indicate structural relevance or continuity preparation, but they remain subordinate to evidence and topology authority.

## 8. Capacity Signals

Capacity signals describe:
- energy availability expansion
- grid reinforcement
- transport throughput expansion
- research throughput expansion
- compute siting readiness indicators

Capacity signals influence readiness interpretation.

They do not establish corridor placement authority.

Capacity signals are particularly relevant to trust, profile, and builder interpretation, but their role remains indicative rather than determinative.

## 9. Constraint Signals

Constraint signals describe:
- regulatory barriers
- transmission bottlenecks
- water availability limits
- policy exclusions
- land-use conflicts

Constraint signals reinforce interpretation boundaries.

They must remain visible when present.

Constraint signals may affect readiness, corridor breadth, or deployment posture, but they do so by clarifying limits rather than by changing canonical classification authority.

## 10. Transition Signals

Transition signals describe:
- movement toward anchor classification
- movement toward corridor attachment stabilization
- movement toward lifecycle advancement
- movement toward expansion-edge participation

Transition signals indicate structural trajectory.

They must not independently move lifecycle state.

They may justify closer review, change-log attention, or future package revision, but they do not perform promotion or reclassification on their own.

## 11. Signal Interaction with Lifecycle

Signal interpretation authority varies by lifecycle stage:
- Draft -> exploratory signals
- Evidence-Attached -> bounded signals
- Corridor-Attached -> topology-visible signals
- Complete -> stable readiness signals
- Canonical -> baseline transition signals
- Superseded -> historical signals only
- Deprecated -> inactive signals

Lifecycle status governs signal interpretive weight.

Signals may become more reliable as lifecycle maturity increases, but their authority boundary remains unchanged.

No lifecycle stage permits signals to replace evidence, topology, or explicit lifecycle status.

## 12. Signal Interaction with Topology Classification

Signals may:
- support topology interpretation
- explain adjacency continuity
- indicate expansion-edge participation
- clarify classification boundaries

Signals must not:
- replace the topology trio
- override completion-layer semantics
- promote adjacency to anchor equivalence
- create new corridor groups

Signals may explain why a jurisdiction appears to be moving toward a different topology posture.

They may not declare that movement complete without canonical support.

## 13. Signal Interaction with Adjacency

Signals may:
- reinforce adjacency interpretation
- indicate strengthening corridor continuity
- support multi-jurisdiction linkage explanation

Signals must not:
- create adjacency classification independently
- override interpretation boundary constraints
- replace adjacency evidence requirements

Adjacency signals are supportive, not constitutive.

They help explain how adjacency may be changing over time without changing the underlying evidence requirements for adjacency participation.

## 14. Signal Visibility Requirements

Signals must appear in:
- `signals.md`

Signals may also appear in:
- `change-log.md` normalization explanations
- renderer helper-layer visibility lifts where permitted

Signals must remain visible when they materially affect interpretation posture.

A renderer may compress, summarize, or reorganize signal presentation under governed renderer rules, but it may not erase materially relevant signals or convert them into stronger authority claims.

## 15. Signal Compliance Definition

A jurisdiction package is signal-compliant when all of the following are true:
- each signal belongs to a defined signal category
- signals remain evidence-bounded
- signals do not override topology classification
- signals do not modify lifecycle status
- signals preserve interpretation boundaries

Non-compliance occurs when any of the following are true:
- signals imply classification authority
- signals replace evidence
- signals override adjacency limits
- signals create corridor placement without canonical support
