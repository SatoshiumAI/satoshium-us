# ATLAS Corridor Definitions v1

## 1. Purpose

This document defines the corridor ontology used by Atlas.

Corridors describe infrastructure continuity structures across jurisdictions.

Corridors organize anchor infrastructure into topology systems.

Corridors enable cross-jurisdiction participation reasoning.

Corridors do not replace topology trio classification.

Corridors must remain evidence-grounded.

Corridors are topology structures.

They are not scorecards, rankings, or signal-only abstractions.

## 2. Corridor Definition

A corridor is a structured infrastructure continuity pathway connecting anchor jurisdictions through energy, compute, research, transport, or transmission infrastructure.

Corridors may include:
- direct anchor jurisdictions
- adjacent support jurisdictions
- federal overlay continuity segments
- cross-border infrastructure segments

Corridors must be evidence-supported.

Signals alone cannot establish corridor existence.

Signals may indicate emerging corridor direction, expansion-edge potential, or continuity preparation.

They may not independently create a corridor.

## 3. Corridor Qualification Criteria

A corridor qualifies when at least one of the following exists:
- shared transmission backbone continuity
- shared compute siting infrastructure chain
- shared research infrastructure network
- shared transport throughput infrastructure
- federal corridor designation
- pipeline continuity supported by anchor infrastructure endpoints

Corridors must have anchor participation.

Adjacency alone cannot originate a corridor.

A corridor may later traverse adjacency-supported jurisdictions, but its existence must remain rooted in evidence-supported anchor structure.

## 4. Corridor Role Types

Atlas recognizes the following corridor participation roles:
- anchor
- transit
- terminus
- support-node
- expansion-edge

Each jurisdiction participating in a corridor must occupy one or more corridor roles.

Roles must remain consistent with completion-layer semantics.

Corridor roles describe how a jurisdiction participates in corridor continuity.

They do not replace the canonical topology trio.

## 5. Anchor Role Definition

Anchor jurisdictions:
- host stabilizing infrastructure
- provide corridor continuity origin strength
- support downstream infrastructure participation

Anchor placement requires direct infrastructure presence.

Adjacency alone cannot establish anchor role.

Anchor role is the strongest corridor participation role in structural terms because it supplies corridor-stabilizing presence rather than downstream continuity alone.

## 6. Transit Role Definition

Transit jurisdictions:
- preserve corridor continuity
- enable infrastructure passage
- support throughput alignment

Transit roles may include adjacency-supported participation.

Transit roles do not imply corridor origin authority.

Transit role means the jurisdiction helps continuity pass through.

It does not mean the jurisdiction originates or stabilizes the corridor in anchor terms.

## 7. Terminus Role Definition

Terminus jurisdictions:
- represent corridor endpoints
- host terminal infrastructure structures
- support downstream branching or boundary transitions

Terminus placement must be evidence-grounded.

Signals alone cannot establish terminus status.

A terminus is a corridor endpoint role, not a weaker form of anchor.

It must be explicitly supported by documented terminal or edge-position structure.

## 8. Support-Node Role Definition

Support-node jurisdictions:
- reinforce corridor continuity
- host supporting infrastructure dependencies
- enable indirect participation

Support-node placement must remain adjacency- or infrastructure-grounded.

Support-node roles must not imply anchor equivalence.

Support-node participation is valid corridor participation, but it is structurally indirect compared with anchor placement.

## 9. Expansion-Edge Role Definition

Expansion-edge jurisdictions:
- represent emerging corridor participation zones
- indicate forward continuity direction
- support pipeline-stage infrastructure alignment

Expansion-edge roles must remain signal-supported or adjacency-supported.

Expansion-edge roles must not imply anchor placement.

An expansion-edge role may describe emerging structural direction without granting settled corridor authority.

## 10. Corridor Chaining Logic

Corridors may traverse multiple jurisdictions without anchor infrastructure in each segment.

Adjacent jurisdictions may preserve corridor continuity.

Support-node jurisdictions may enable corridor traversal.

Expansion-edge jurisdictions may indicate corridor extension direction.

Corridor chains must preserve anchor visibility.

A long corridor chain must still make clear where the corridor’s anchor structure resides.

Adjacency-supported traversal must not erase or blur the underlying anchor basis of the corridor.

## 11. Multi-Corridor Participation

Jurisdictions may participate in multiple corridors simultaneously.

Multi-corridor participation must:
- preserve topology trio classification
- preserve completion-layer semantics
- maintain corridor-role clarity
- avoid corridor-role conflicts

Corridor coexistence must remain explicitly documented.

Multiple corridor roles may coexist only when the canonical package clearly distinguishes them.

## 12. Multi-Foundation Corridor Coexistence

Jurisdictions may participate across multiple infrastructure foundations including:
- energy corridors
- compute corridors
- research corridors
- transport corridors
- federal infrastructure corridors

Cross-foundation participation must remain classification-consistent.

Foundation differences must remain visible.

Multi-foundation coexistence may be real, but it must not collapse into an unbounded mixed-role interpretation that obscures dominant placement or supporting roles.

## 13. Adjacency-Supported Corridor Continuity

Corridor continuity may traverse jurisdictions without anchor infrastructure through adjacency participation.

Adjacency-supported segments must remain evidence-grounded.

Adjacency-supported segments must preserve interpretation boundary visibility.

Adjacency continuity must not imply anchor equivalence.

Adjacency allows corridor traversal without requiring direct anchor infrastructure in every participating jurisdiction.

It does not erase the distinction between support and origin.

## 14. Federal Overlay Corridors

Federal infrastructure may define corridor continuity structures.

Examples may include:
- interstate transmission systems
- national research facilities
- defense infrastructure corridors
- federal compute infrastructure networks

Federal overlays must remain evidence-documented.

Federal overlays must not independently establish anchor placement.

Federal continuity may strengthen corridor reasoning, but it does not automatically upgrade all participating jurisdictions to anchor status.

## 15. Cross-Border Corridor Continuity

Corridors may extend across national boundaries.

Cross-border participation may include:
- transmission continuity
- pipeline continuity
- research infrastructure alignment
- transport throughput continuity

Cross-border continuity must preserve interpretation boundaries.

Cross-border continuity does not imply classification equivalence.

Cross-border segments may participate structurally without collapsing national boundary distinctions or topology authority differences.

## 16. Corridor Origin Visibility Rule

Corridors must preserve identifiable origin structure.

Multi-segment corridor chains must maintain anchor visibility.

Corridor origin must not be obscured by adjacency-only traversal.

If a corridor narrative no longer makes clear where anchor structure exists, the corridor interpretation is non-compliant.

## 17. Corridor Terminus Visibility Rule

Terminus placement must remain explicitly documented.

Transit participation must not be interpreted as terminus placement.

Signals alone must not establish terminus status.

A corridor endpoint must be supported by evidence or governed canonical normalization logic, not by directional implication alone.

## 18. Corridor Distance Sensitivity Rule

Corridor accessibility statements must preserve distance-aware interpretation.

Multi-jurisdiction traversal must not be described as local participation without supporting infrastructure proximity evidence.

Distance-sensitive interpretation must remain visible.

A jurisdiction may participate in corridor continuity while still remaining structurally distant from anchor infrastructure.

That distance must not be collapsed into direct-local equivalence.

## 19. Corridor Lifecycle Interaction

Corridor participation authority varies by lifecycle stage:
- Draft -> exploratory corridor participation
- Evidence-Attached -> bounded corridor interpretation
- Corridor-Attached -> topology-visible corridor participation
- Complete -> stable corridor placement
- Canonical -> baseline corridor continuity reference
- Superseded -> historical corridor participation
- Deprecated -> inactive corridor participation

Lifecycle stage affects corridor interpretive authority.

It does not redefine corridor ontology.

A corridor role may remain the same while the package’s authority to function as a reference changes with lifecycle maturity.

## 20. Corridor Compliance Definition

A jurisdiction package is corridor-compliant when all of the following are true:
- corridor roles remain evidence-grounded
- adjacency participation remains bounded
- anchor placement remains infrastructure-supported
- expansion-edge placement remains signal-supported
- terminus placement remains explicit
- corridor chaining preserves origin visibility

Non-compliance occurs when any of the following are true:
- corridor roles are inferred from signals alone
- adjacency is upgraded to anchor placement
- terminus placement is implied without documentation
- corridor continuity obscures anchor structure
- foundation roles are merged without visibility
