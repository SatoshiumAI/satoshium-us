# Nigeria — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly visible sources only, principally Nigerian government portals, regulators, public operators, public institutional websites, and stable official-site search-visible records where direct extraction was limited by bot protection, dynamic rendering, DNS failures, hosting issues, or other intermittent availability. It documents observable administrative structures, infrastructure layers, continuity systems, interoperability mechanisms, and operational coordination environments visible in Nigeria.

The interpretation here is intentionally operational and infrastructure-oriented. It does not rank Nigeria, assign readiness or trust scores, infer hidden capability, infer surveillance capability, infer military or intelligence posture, create geopolitical interpretation, create West Africa or Africa leadership interpretation, create regional-power interpretation, create oil-and-gas interpretation, create population-size interpretation, create economic interpretation, or apply fintech, mobile-money, cultural-industry, gateway, or strategic-geography narratives. Public evidence is treated as bounded and incomplete by default, and the wording remains renderer-safe by limiting claims to what public sources directly expose.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Publicly visible state infrastructure in Nigeria presents as an Abuja-centered federal administrative environment with distributed state and sectoral continuity layers. The federal services portal publicly states that Nigeria has thirty-six states and that Abuja is the capital. The Office of the Secretary to the Government of the Federation publicly locates the SGF at the Shehu Shagari Complex in the Three Arms Zone, Abuja, and publicly exposes agencies directly supervised by the SGF. This supports a visible federal coordination layer concentrated in Abuja-facing institutions.

A second public-service layer is distributed across ministries, departments, agencies, and service portals rather than being confined to a single office. OSGF public materials identify directly supervised agencies including the Federal Road Safety Corps, National Agency for Science and Engineering Infrastructure, National Boundary Commission, Bureau of Public Service Reforms, ServiCom, Fiscal Responsibility Commission, and Infrastructure Concession Regulatory Commission. Search-visible federal civil service materials also expose a ministries, departments, and agencies listing surface. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as an Abuja-centered federal administrative environment with distributed state and sectoral continuity, rather than as a single-node public-service stack.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

Nigeria’s publicly visible identity layer is anchored by the National Identity Management Commission. Official NIMC search-visible records present the commission as the public surface for managing the National Identification Number, enrolment, and digital identity services. Separate official NIMC records publicly describe the National Identification Number as a unique number issued after enrolment and used to match a person to biometric and other details in the National Identity Database. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a formal national identity-administration layer without inferring any non-public surveillance or verification capability.

A second visible layer exists through federal digital-service access surfaces. The federal services portal publicly presents Nigeria’s government-services gateway and exposes NIMC as a service provider with public pages for NIN pre-enrolment and self-service modification workflows. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as an identity-and-service environment with national enrolment, NIN issuance, identity-data update surfaces, and federal online service access, while deeper backend identity-validation architecture remains only partly visible.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

Nigeria’s publicly visible payment infrastructure is organized around the Central Bank of Nigeria and the national payments system. CBN publicly states that its payments-system supervision mandate is to ensure the soundness and safety of the payments system through rules and guidelines. Official CBN search-visible materials identify banks, discount houses, the Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System, the Nigeria Stock Exchange, payment service providers, and switching companies as key players in the Nigerian payments system under central oversight. CBN public materials also expose standing national payment initiatives including NUBAN, cheque truncation, the National Central Switch, the Nigeria Electronic Fraud Forum, and RTGS deployment. This is direct public evidence of a centrally coordinated payment environment.

The large-value settlement layer is clearly visible through CBN’s RTGS and Scripless Securities Settlement System. CBN publicly states that RTGS operations commenced in December 2006, that a replacement RTGS system and central securities depository were deployed in December 2013, and that the RTGS and S4 environment was upgraded in April 2023 with ISO 20022 support, API integration with other financial market infrastructures, and four settlement sessions. CBN’s public payment-modes materials also identify NEFT and NIBSS Instant Payment within the non-cash payment environment.

Retail and interbank interoperability are additionally visible through NIBSS and the CBN licensing structure. Official NIBSS search-visible records describe NIBSS as an inter-bank settlement system serving licensed banks, describe NIBSS Instant Payment as a deferred net settlement system in which funds become available in the beneficiary account in real time before settlement, and expose the Nigeria Central Switch as a public switching and interconnectivity layer. CBN’s public payment-service-provider directory further exposes licensed card schemes, switching and processing licensees, mobile-money operator licensees, and payment solution service providers. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a payment-settlement interoperability environment with central-bank oversight, visible interbank rails, switching infrastructure, and licensed provider layers, without converting those layers into fintech or economic-power narratives.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Telecommunications in Nigeria are publicly visible as a regulated communications environment. The Nigerian Communications Commission publicly describes itself as responsible for creating an enabling environment for competition among operators and for ensuring provision of qualitative and efficient telecommunications services throughout the country. This makes the regulatory layer directly observable even where deeper carrier topology is not public.

A second visible layer is country-domain administration and internet-exchange infrastructure. NiRA publicly states that it is the registry for the .ng country-code top-level domain, that it maintains the registry database for registered .ng names, and that it operates a registry-registrar-registrant model rather than direct end-user registration. Official IXPN search-visible records describe the Internet Exchange Point of Nigeria as a neutral, membership-based, not-for-profit organization, while IXPN traffic-statistics surfaces publicly expose exchange activity across multiple Lagos exchange locations. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a telecommunications-continuity environment with formal sector regulation, visible .ng domain administration, and internet-exchange infrastructure, while private backbone engineering, full carrier interconnection maps, and enterprise-network topology remain only partly visible.

## Electricity & Energy Infrastructure

Nigeria’s publicly visible electricity environment is organized around transmission, regulation, and market-pool coordination. The Transmission Company of Nigeria publicly presents itself as a transmission and system-operations company based in Abuja and explicitly distinguishes itself from distribution companies that supply electricity directly to homes. TCN public materials also tie its role to the post-2005 power-sector reform framework. Search-visible NERC materials publicly state that the Nigerian Electricity Regulatory Commission was established by the Electricity Act 2023 as an independent regulatory body with the mandate to regulate the Nigerian Electricity Supply Industry. This establishes visible transmission-system and regulatory layers.

A second visible layer exists through market-pool administration. NBET publicly states that the Nigerian Bulk Electricity Trading Plc is the manager and administrator of the electricity pool in the Nigerian electricity supply industry and that it is wholly owned by the Federal Government of Nigeria. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as an electricity-coordination environment with transmission-system operations, a standing regulator, and a visible electricity-pool administration layer. Detailed distribution-company topology, dispatch logic, reserve practice, and full commercial contracting structure remain only partly visible in public sources.

## Transportation Infrastructure

Nigeria’s publicly visible transport environment is organized through a national ministry layer and separate road and rail continuity layers. The Federal Ministry of Transportation publicly exposes departments for rail transport services, road transport and mass transit administration, maritime services, and transport planning and coordination. Official ministry materials describe the transport-planning and coordination department as liaising with ministry departments and agencies and collaborating with other ministries, departments, and agencies on national issues. This is direct public evidence of a standing transport-coordination layer.

Road continuity is also visible through the Federal Ministry of Works and the Federal Roads Maintenance Agency. Official search-visible Federal Ministry of Works materials locate the ministry headquarters in Abuja and describe a continuing federal roads and road-infrastructure role. Official ministry-linked records identify FERMA under the supervision of the Federal Ministry of Works. FERMA search-visible materials describe the agency as Nigeria’s institutional mechanism for monitoring and maintaining federal roads, while FERMA’s public site exposes operational road-maintenance departments and emergency contact surfaces.

Rail continuity is publicly visible through the Nigerian Railway Corporation. NRC publicly presents the railway system as the oldest modern form of transportation in Nigeria, describes a 1067 mm Cape-gauge track system, and exposes passenger, excursion, and freight services together with current operational notices, including Lagos-Ibadan service updates. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria’s transportation environment as a layered road-and-rail continuity system coordinated through national ministry structures, without converting those layers into gateway or corridor narratives.

## Aviation Infrastructure

Nigeria’s aviation infrastructure is publicly visible through the Nigeria Civil Aviation Authority, the Federal Airports Authority of Nigeria, and the Nigerian Airspace Management Agency. NCAA public materials describe the authority as responsible for aviation safety and economic regulation and expose regulatory, advisory, and passenger-rights surfaces. NCAA’s public site also presents a national civil-aviation environment that includes airports, airstrips, heliports, operators, and licensed aviation personnel. This is sufficient to identify a standing aviation oversight layer.

Airport continuity is visibly organized through FAAN. FAAN publicly states that it manages all commercial airports in Nigeria and locates its head office on the grounds of Nnamdi Azikiwe International Airport, Abuja. FAAN’s public international-airports materials identify Murtala Muhammed International Airport in Lagos, Nnamdi Azikiwe International Airport in Abuja, Mallam Aminu Kano International Airport, Port Harcourt International Airport, and Akanu Ibiam International Airport in Enugu as part of the public international-airport layer. Official FAAN search-visible records also state that the international and cargo terminals at Murtala Muhammed International Airport operate on a 24-hour basis.

Air-navigation continuity is publicly visible through NAMA. NAMA publicly states that it was established by Act No. 48 of 1999, and official search-visible NAMA materials describe it as the air navigation service provider responsible for managing Nigerian airspace in line with ICAO standards and recommended practices. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria’s aviation environment as a regulated airport-and-air-navigation system with visible oversight, airport-management, and airspace-management layers, without applying aviation-hub rhetoric.

## Maritime & Port Infrastructure

Nigeria’s maritime and port environment is publicly visible through the Nigerian Ports Authority. NPA publicly states that it is the federal government agency that governs and operates ports in Nigeria and identifies major ports including Lagos Port Complex, Tin Can Island Port Complex, Calabar Port Complex, Delta Ports, Rivers Port Complex, Onne Port Complex, and Lekki Deep Sea Port. NPA also publicly locates its head office at Marina, Lagos. This establishes a directly observable port-administration layer and makes the Lagos, Tin Can, Rivers, and Onne port nodes publicly visible.

A second visible layer exists through inland waterways administration. NIWA publicly states that it was established under the Laws of the Federation of Nigeria and was given primary responsibility to improve and develop Nigeria’s inland waterways for navigation. NIWA’s public materials also connect this role to inland-waterway connectivity and intermodal transport support.

A third visible layer is maritime safety and regulatory administration. NIMASA publicly states that it was established for maritime safety, seafarers standards, maritime labour, shipping regulation, cabotage administration, pollution prevention and control, port and flag state duties, and implementation of domesticated IMO and ILO conventions. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a maritime continuity environment with port administration, inland-waterway administration, and maritime-safety and shipping-regulation layers, while private terminal arrangements and commercial cargo-routing detail remain only partly visible.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

Nigeria’s public emergency-coordination layer is visible through the National Emergency Management Agency. Official NEMA search-visible records state that the agency was established to manage disasters in Nigeria and describe its mission as coordinating resources for disaster prevention, preparedness, mitigation, and response. This is sufficient to identify a formal national emergency-management and coordination layer.

Public NEMA materials also show ongoing operational coordination. The NEMA site publicly exposes incident and preparedness updates including fire-risk cautions, climate-risk early-warning activity, multi-agency response to a market fire in Kano, and post-flood assessment activity in Lagos. Search-visible NEMA materials additionally expose territorial-office surfaces. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria’s disaster-response environment as a formal national coordination system with visible warning, assessment, and multi-agency response functions, while full resource inventories and all state-level emergency dependencies remain outside full public visibility.

## Cybersecurity & Data Infrastructure

Nigeria’s publicly visible cyber-governance layer includes NITDA, ngCERT, the National Cybersecurity Coordination Centre, and the Nigeria Data Protection Commission. Official NITDA search-visible materials describe the agency’s mandate as operating and implementing national IT policy and creating a framework for planning, research, development, standardization, application, coordination, monitoring, evaluation, and regulation of information-technology practices in Nigeria. This provides a public administrative layer for national IT governance.

Public cyber-incident coordination is also visible. Official ngCERT search-visible materials describe the Nigeria Computer Emergency Response Team as established to manage cyber-threat risks in Nigeria’s cyberspace and to coordinate incident response and mitigation strategies. Public ngCERT RFC2350 records additionally expose a 24/7 monitored operational contact surface. Official NCCC search-visible records identify the centre as an official federal government site and publicly expose cybersecurity-coordination messaging around critical national information infrastructure protection, although direct page extraction was limited at retrieval time.

A data-governance layer is publicly visible through the Nigeria Data Protection Commission. Official NDPC search-visible materials describe the commission as Nigeria’s data protection authority under the Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, and publicly state that it safeguards privacy, enforces regulations, and promotes responsible data handling. NDPC also publicly exposes an information-management portal for data-controller, data-processor, and compliance-organization registration workflows. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as having a public cyber-coordination and data-governance environment, while deeper defensive tooling, incident-response methods, and non-public cyber-operational capability remain outside public observability.

## Research & Education Network Infrastructure

Nigeria’s research and education network layer is publicly visible through NgREN. NgREN publicly states that it was incorporated as a private company limited by guarantee on 21 February 2012 and that it provides connectivity infrastructure for service delivery to its members. NgREN also publicly states that it assumes legal and technical responsibilities to ICANN and AfriNIC while operating within Nigerian corporate law. This establishes a formal national research-and-education networking layer.

A second visible layer exists through institutional services and federation-oriented connectivity. NgREN publicly exposes high-speed internet connectivity, capacity-building services, eduroam, the NIRIS project, and high-performance computing support. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a research-network-supported environment with academic connectivity, institutional interoperability, and research-network service layers, while campus-by-campus topology remains only partly visible.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

Nigeria’s regional and international connectivity is publicly visible across domain administration, internet exchange, research networking, aviation, and maritime administration. NiRA administers the .ng country-code domain environment, while IXPN publicly exposes interconnection infrastructure and traffic-statistics surfaces. NgREN publicly states that it operates with ICANN and AfriNIC responsibilities and exposes eduroam service that is designed for participating institutions in Nigeria and internationally. These are direct public indicators of external-facing network and institutional interconnection layers.

International transport continuity is also directly visible. FAAN publicly identifies a multi-airport international aviation layer spanning Abuja, Lagos, Kano, Port Harcourt, and Enugu. NPA publicly identifies a multi-port seaport environment spanning Lagos, Tin Can, Calabar, Delta, Rivers, and Onne, while NIMASA publicly identifies implementation of domesticated IMO and ILO conventions. In the payment sector, the public materials reviewed here are stronger for national interoperability and settlement than for specifically named ECOWAS or wider regional payment schemes. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a regional and international interconnection environment through visible airports, seaports, exchange infrastructure, ccTLD administration, and research-network interfaces, without converting those interfaces into geopolitical, gateway, or regional-power narratives.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of Nigeria as both an Abuja-centered and territorially distributed continuity environment. Administrative concentration is visible through Abuja’s status as the federal capital and through Abuja-facing institutional surfaces including the OSGF, the federal services portal, TCN, NERC-related public materials, FAAN headquarters, and other federal regulators and agencies. This establishes a clear federal coordination concentration in Abuja.

At the same time, critical continuity is not confined to Abuja. Lagos continuity is publicly visible through the NPA head office, Lagos Port Complex, Tin Can Island Port Complex, Murtala Muhammed International Airport, NRC Lagos-Ibadan operations, and IXPN’s publicly visible Lagos exchange locations. Additional territorial continuity is visible through Port Harcourt and Onne maritime infrastructure in Rivers State, international airport nodes in Kano, Port Harcourt, and Enugu, FERMA’s road-maintenance structure, NEMA’s incident activity in states including Kano and Lagos, and TCN’s publicly visible regional transmission activity outside the capital. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Nigeria as a distributed territorial continuity environment in which Abuja-centered administration coexists with multiple airport, port, rail, road, electricity, and service-delivery nodes across the federation’s thirty-six-state environment.

## Structural Constraints & Evidence Boundaries

This file is public-source-bound. Telecom visibility is incomplete, especially for private backbone routes, full carrier interconnection maps, enterprise connectivity, and deeper commercial operator topology. Private-network visibility is incomplete across banking, telecommunications, enterprise, port, airport, and government-contractor environments. Cyber-operational visibility is incomplete beyond the public existence of NITDA, ngCERT, NCCC, NDPC, and their published mandates or public-facing coordination surfaces. Logistics visibility is incomplete for private freight routing, warehousing, inland-distribution practice, and non-public contingency procedures. Commercial-topology visibility is incomplete for bank-to-bank dependencies beyond published rails, private telecom peering, terminal operating arrangements, and backend service-provider dependencies. Electricity-market visibility is incomplete for full dispatch logic, balancing practice, and detailed contractual structure beyond publicly identified institutions.

Several relevant official pages were dynamically rendered, intermittently inaccessible, bot-protected, DNS-limited, suspended, or otherwise inconsistent at retrieval time, so some narrow points rely on stable official-site search-visible text rather than full direct extraction. Bounded observability therefore applies throughout. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. This evidence layer does not infer undocumented resilience, undisclosed surveillance architecture, non-public cyber capability, or deeper logistics, electricity-market, or commercial structure beyond what public-source materials directly expose.
