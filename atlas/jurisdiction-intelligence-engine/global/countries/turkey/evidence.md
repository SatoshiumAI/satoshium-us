# Turkey — Evidence Layer

## Overview

Turkey functions as an Ankara-centered administrative and nationally distributed infrastructure environment whose publicly visible continuity depends on coordinated rail, motorway, airport, port, electricity-transmission, gas-transmission, digital-service, payment, cybersecurity, and emergency-management layers rather than on any single system. Public materials from TEİAŞ, BOTAŞ, KGM, TCDD, DHMİ, BTK, the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye, e-Devlet, AFAD, and USOM show a multi-system structure in which national coordination remains highly visible at the state level.

At the same time, Turkey’s continuity is not confined to the capital. Public operator and regulator materials show strong Istanbul concentration in aviation, capital-market infrastructure, and parts of the commercial and electronic-certificate environment, while transport, energy, public-service, and emergency systems remain distributed across the national territory. Publicly documented infrastructure also shows Turkey operating through Black Sea, Aegean, and Mediterranean interfaces, Thrace-facing land continuity, eastern gas-entry corridors, and research-network links to broader international systems.

## Geographic & Regional Position

Turkey is a sovereign state with a land-connected Anatolian core and a European territorial component in Thrace. It borders Greece and Bulgaria to the west, Georgia, Armenia, and Azerbaijan through Nakhchivan to the east and northeast, and Iran, Iraq, and Syria to the south and southeast. It also has direct maritime interfaces with the Black Sea, Aegean Sea, and Mediterranean Sea.

This geography makes domestic territorial continuity and border-facing infrastructure operationally significant in visible ways. Public road, rail, air-navigation, electricity, and gas materials describe a system that must maintain continuity across a large national territory while also managing western, northern, eastern, and southern interfaces. In Istanbul specifically, public Marmaray materials make Bosphorus-crossing rail continuity directly visible as part of the national transport system rather than as a symbolic geographic feature.

## Transportation & Logistics Infrastructure

Rail is a core public infrastructure layer. TCDD publishes the national railway operator’s official site and annual network-statement materials, while TCDD Taşımacılık publishes passenger-service materials including Ankara-Istanbul and Ankara-Konya high-speed-train services. Public Marmaray materials further show a rail-tube-crossing and station system under the Bosphorus, making cross-strait passenger and freight continuity operationally visible within the wider rail network.

Road continuity is publicly organized through the General Directorate of Highways (KGM). KGM states that, as of 1 January 2026, the road network under its responsibility totals 68,517 km and consists of motorways, state roads, and provincial roads. The same materials list 3,796 km of motorways and 28,297 km of divided roads, indicating a nationwide road layer that supports internal continuity across the territory rather than a single-corridor structure.

Aviation continuity is publicly organized through DHMİ. DHMİ states that it is the responsible authority for the provision of air traffic services within the entire territory of Turkey, including its territorial waters and the airspace over the high seas within the Ankara and Istanbul FIRs. Public airport materials also show a layered airport system in which Istanbul Airport and Istanbul Sabiha Gökçen form the clearest visible commercial aviation concentration, while Ankara Esenboğa remains the capital-area airport within the national airport network published by DHMİ.

Maritime continuity is publicly visible through major port operators and maritime-facing infrastructure. Official Mersin International Port materials describe a Mediterranean port environment with multimodal and railway-linked hinterland access, while public operator materials emphasize its role in container and cargo handling. Publicly available transport evidence therefore supports normalization of Turkey as a layered logistics environment combining a national railway structure, high-speed passenger rail, motorway and divided-road continuity, Bosphorus-crossing rail infrastructure, state-managed air-navigation space, large Istanbul airport concentration, and maritime interfaces on multiple coasts.

## Energy & Industrial Structure

Turkey’s electricity backbone is publicly organized through TEİAŞ. TEİAŞ’s electricity-transmission materials describe a national high-voltage transmission environment with 817 high-voltage substations and 15 interconnection lines with neighboring countries as of April 2026. Public TEİAŞ materials also identify a centrally coordinated transmission structure whose role includes national dispatch and interconnection management rather than isolated regional grids.

Gas transmission is publicly organized through BOTAŞ. BOTAŞ materials describe the Russian Federation-Türkiye main transmission line entering Turkey at Malkoçlar on the Bulgarian border and extending to Ankara through the Thrace-Istanbul-İzmit-Bursa-Eskişehir route. The same official materials describe the Blue Stream line from Samsun to Ankara, the Azerbaijan-Türkiye and Baku-Tbilisi-Erzurum gas flows via Georgia, the East Anatolian main transmission line for eastern gas imports, Tuz Gölü underground storage, Silivri underground storage, and LNG/FSRU infrastructure including the Marmara Ereğlisi LNG terminal and the Dörtyol FSRU terminal.

Additional public BOTAŞ materials also document TANAP and TurkStream as interconnection layers within Turkey’s gas system. Official TANAP materials describe the project as carrying Shah Deniz Phase II gas first to Turkey and then onward through Turkey, while official TurkStream materials describe Black Sea and Thrace-based infrastructure that entered service for gas transfer to Turkey and Bulgaria. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Turkey’s energy structure as a centrally coordinated electricity-and-gas environment with multiple import routes, storage sites, LNG/FSRU assets, and explicit cross-border interconnection functions.

## Digital & Telecommunications Infrastructure

Turkey’s digital and telecommunications environment is publicly structured through national regulation, domain administration, electronic-certification services, and a separate academic-network layer. BTK publicly lists the country’s electronic certificate service providers and states that qualified electronic certificates used for secure electronic signatures are obtained through the listed providers. The list itself shows a nationally regulated trust-service environment with both public and private operators.

Public TRABİS materials identify the ".tr" domain-name system as a real-time domain application and management system operated by BTK. This makes national internet naming administration visible as a centrally managed regulatory and operational layer rather than a purely informal or private arrangement.

A separate research-and-education network layer is visible through TÜBİTAK ULAKBİM and ULAKNET. Public ULAKBİM service materials describe ULAKNET as the national academic-network environment, while ULAKNET management materials describe a web-based operational system for institutions connected to the network. Public ULAKBİM materials also indicate GÉANT linkage, showing that part of Turkey’s digital infrastructure is organized through research-network interoperability in addition to commercial telecommunications.

Public visibility is materially weaker for detailed commercial peering, internet-exchange, backbone, and data-center topology than for domain administration, trust services, and research networking. Accordingly, this evidence layer does not infer a hidden commercial exchange structure beyond what is clearly visible from BTK, TRABİS, and ULAKBİM-related public materials.

## Financial & Payment Infrastructure

Turkey’s payment infrastructure is publicly centered on the Central Bank of the Republic of Türkiye. Official central-bank materials identify the EFT system and FAST as core payment rails, while FAST’s public site describes a fast, easy, secure, 7/24 instant-payment environment integrated with participating financial institutions. Public FAST materials also describe layered services and broad participant coverage across banks and payment or electronic-money institutions.

This produces a payment structure in which central-bank-operated systems remain the principal visible coordination layer for domestic transfers and settlement. Public materials therefore support normalization of Turkey’s payment environment as a centrally operated system in which instant and non-instant payment rails interact through the central bank rather than through a fully decentralized market structure.

Turkey’s capital-markets infrastructure adds a second publicly visible financial layer concentrated in Istanbul. Borsa İstanbul states that it brings together the exchanges operating in Turkish capital markets under a single roof and exists to provide organized, transparent, secure, and efficient trading environments for capital-markets instruments, foreign currencies, precious metals, and related assets. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Istanbul as the clearest visible capital-markets concentration point within the national financial system.

## Government & Administrative Technology Structure

Turkey’s public-service access layer is visibly organized around e-Devlet. Official e-Devlet materials describe the platform as a single point through which users can access information and documents and carry out application processes, while also exposing mobile access for major consumer devices. This indicates a centralized public-service gateway rather than a fragmented collection of unrelated agency front ends.

Administrative technology also intersects with the national trust-service environment. BTK’s electronic-certificate-service-provider materials show a regulated structure for qualified electronic certificates used in secure electronic signatures, creating a visible support layer for authenticated digital transactions. Public materials therefore support normalization of Turkey’s administrative-technology structure as a layered environment built around a central service gateway plus regulated digital-signature infrastructure.

The public record also shows that this layer is not isolated from other systems. e-Devlet access, payment rails, certified electronic-signature infrastructure, and sectoral regulators together form a multi-layer administrative-access environment whose continuity depends on coordination between digital identity, communications regulation, and state service delivery.

## Regional & International Interoperability Infrastructure

Turkey’s infrastructure is publicly documented as interoperable across transport, energy, aviation, digital, and research-network layers. In transport, TCDD network-statement materials, TCDD Taşımacılık passenger services, KGM’s national road network, Marmaray’s Bosphorus rail crossing, and DHMİ’s Ankara and Istanbul FIR structure all show a transport system with national coordination and explicit international-facing interfaces.

Energy interoperability is especially clear. TEİAŞ publishes interconnection-line counts with neighboring countries, while BOTAŞ documents gas-entry and transmission relationships through Bulgaria, Georgia, the Black Sea, eastern land entries, TANAP’s connection into the Turkish transmission system, and TurkStream’s connection toward Bulgaria. These are operational interconnection functions rather than abstract regional claims.

Digital interoperability is visible more narrowly but still clearly through the research-network layer. ULAKNET’s public linkage to GÉANT shows that part of Turkey’s digital environment operates through research and education network interoperability. Public TRABİS and BTK materials likewise show nationally administered internet naming and trust-service functions that can support broader digital-system interaction, even where detailed commercial backbone interoperability remains only partially visible.

## Disaster Resilience, Cybersecurity & Operational Coordination

Turkey’s public disaster-management layer is centered on AFAD. AFAD’s public materials describe the authority as responsible for disaster and emergency management, including prevention, damage minimization, and coordination of post-disaster response. Public AFAD materials also describe a Disaster and Emergency Coordination Board responsible for assessing information during disasters and emergencies, identifying measures to be taken, ensuring implementation, and coordinating public agencies, organizations, and NGOs.

AFAD’s public English-language materials also expose a broader institutional continuity layer including civil-defence search-and-rescue directorates and training facilities. This supports normalization of Turkey’s resilience environment as one that combines central coordination with distributed field structures rather than relying only on capital-based command.

The public cyber-response layer is visible through USOM. USOM’s public materials show a continuing national cyber-notification and technical-service environment including CVE applications, cyber reports, malicious-link lists, and security notifications, even as some functions transition to the Cybersecurity Presidency’s newer institutional structure. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Turkey’s cyber and continuity layer as an operational coordination environment with both disaster-management and cyber-incident components.

## Regional & International Connectivity

Turkey’s regional and international connectivity is layered rather than singular. Bulgaria-facing continuity is directly visible in BOTAŞ materials through the Malkoçlar entry point and through TurkStream-related transfer toward Bulgaria. Georgia-facing continuity is directly visible in BOTAŞ materials describing Shah Deniz and Baku-Tbilisi-Erzurum-linked gas flows via Georgia. Greece-facing operational continuity is visible more conservatively through Thrace-side road and rail approaches and through TANAP’s official connection logic toward the Greek border at İpsala.

Black Sea connectivity is visible through Blue Stream, TurkStream, Samsun-linked gas infrastructure, and DHMİ’s air-navigation documentation for the Ankara and Istanbul FIR structure. Aegean and Mediterranean continuity are visible through the national airport and maritime layers, including Istanbul aviation concentration and Mediterranean port operations such as Mersin. These interfaces are infrastructure-facing and operational rather than geopolitical in character.

Research-network connectivity is visible through ULAKNET’s public linkage to GÉANT. Taken together, the public record shows Turkey connected through land, sea, air, energy, and research-network systems across multiple directions at once, without reducing that connectivity to a single narrative or single corridor.

## Structural Observations

Public evidence supports normalization of Turkey as an Ankara-centered administrative and coordination environment supported by distributed territorial infrastructure. Ankara concentration is visible across national authorities and coordinating bodies in transport, energy, communications regulation, disaster management, and payment infrastructure. That concentration does not eliminate the importance of wider territorial systems, because road, rail, airport, gas, electricity, maritime, and emergency infrastructures are publicly documented as nationwide or multi-region systems.

A second structural feature is Istanbul-centered concentration in commercial, financial, airport, and some digital-adjacent functions. Publicly visible examples include Borsa İstanbul, Istanbul Airport, Sabiha Gökçen Airport, Bosphorus-crossing Marmaray operations, and part of the private electronic-certificate environment. A third structural feature is layered interoperability across transport, energy, digital, and research systems, especially where Turkey’s public infrastructure record exposes border-facing gas routes, interconnection lines, FIR structures, and GÉANT-linked academic networking.

Overall, Turkey presents in public evidence as a layered infrastructure environment in which Ankara-centered administration, Istanbul-centered commercial and airport concentration, distributed territorial continuity, Bosphorus-crossing rail continuity, energy interconnection, payment centralization, digital public-service access, research-network support, and disaster-response and cyber-coordination layers operate as mutually reinforcing systems.

## Constraints & Structural Limitations

This evidence layer is bounded by public observability. It relies on official or operator-published materials from Turkish authorities, state operators, regulated entities, and public-service platforms. These sources describe institutional roles, network scale, corridor participation, and service structure more clearly than they describe real-time redundancy, traffic engineering, stress thresholds, internal contingency procedures, commercial data-center distribution, or private telecom-backbone design.

Visibility is uneven across sectors. Electricity transmission, gas transmission, roads, parts of rail, air-navigation governance, e-government, electronic-signature regulation, and central-bank payment systems are comparatively visible. By contrast, commercial peering topology, private backbone structure, detailed exchange architecture, some port-operating details, and some rail-operational interfaces are less uniformly exposed through stable public pages. Several public sources are dynamically rendered, cookie-gated, partially translated, or inconsistently extractable, which further limits directly quotable detail.

Accordingly, absence of evidence in this file should be treated as bounded public visibility, not as evidence of absence. No hidden-capability inference is intended, and no military, intelligence, or undisclosed strategic capability is inferred from partial public documentation.
