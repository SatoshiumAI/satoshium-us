# Qatar - Evidence Layer

## Strategic Position

- QatarEnergy materials document Qatar as a natural-gas and LNG coordination jurisdiction centered on North Field expansion and large-scale liquefaction growth.
- MCIT, Hukoomi, TASMU, and Lusail materials document a centralized, state-coordinated infrastructure environment in which digital-government, smart-city, and national digital-platform functions are organized through named public programs and institutional operators.
- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results document a central-bank and payment-system coordination surface spanning interbank settlement, retail card-processing infrastructure, mobile payments, and instant-payment services.
- CRA, QNBN, QIXP, Ooredoo, and Vodafone Qatar materials document a telecommunications and digital-infrastructure coordination environment that includes national fiber infrastructure, internet exchange services, regulated duct access, subsea-landing regulation, and 5G rollout.
- Google Cloud, Microsoft, MEEZA, TASMU, and Lusail materials document a digital-government and smart-infrastructure environment with local cloud-region presence, data-center capacity, and named data-platform surfaces.
- Mwani Qatar, Hamad International Airport, and cargo-related materials document a logistics and global connectivity hub spanning maritime freight, aviation cargo, and LNG-linked export connectivity.
- NCSA materials surfaced via official site search results and Google Cloud’s Qatar NIA compliance materials document a cybersecurity coordination jurisdiction built around national assurance and certification structures.
- CRA, QIXP, QatarEnergy, and Qatar Foundation materials place Qatar inside GCC and wider global institutional networks for data exchange, energy trade, logistics, and research collaboration.

## Digital Governance and Identity Infrastructure

- MCIT’s Digital Agenda 2030 documents six national strategic pillars, including digital infrastructure, digital government, and digital technologies, placing digital-governance coordination inside an explicit national transformation program.
- MCIT states that TASMU Smart Qatar develops critical national digital infrastructure and enables data sharing across priority sectors through initiatives including TASMU Smart Solutions, TASMU Platform, TASMU Innovation Lab, TASMU Accelerator, and TASMU Digital Valley.
- MCIT states that the TASMU Platform is a cloud-based national digital platform and central hub for building, testing, and deploying smart solutions across sectors, and describes it as a component of Qatar’s digital infrastructure.
- MCIT materials surfaced via official site search results identify Hukoomi as Qatar’s official e-government portal and describe its role as making government services more efficient, effective, and accessible to citizens, residents, visitors, and businesses.
- Hukoomi and related Qatar Digital Government Strategy materials surfaced via official site search results identify the National Authentication System (Tawtheeq) as part of the digital-government stack, and strategy materials reference Tawtheeq among whole-of-government digital systems.
- Hukoomi news materials surfaced via official site search results describe Tawtheeq as an access layer used by multiple government entities for online-service entry.
- Lusail materials document an integrated ICT environment with city-wide Wi‑Fi, a fibre-optic network, and a Lusail Command & Control Centre that centralizes smart-service monitoring through an operation center and a data center.
- The current source set documents named identity, portal, and smart-city systems, but it does not support broader interoperability claims beyond the systems and uses described in the cited institutional materials.

## Financial Infrastructure and Payment Systems

- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results describe the Financial Instruments and Payment Systems Sector as housing a dedicated Payment Systems Department with high-value payment systems, retail payment systems, analytical studies, and technical-support functions.
- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results describe the Qatar Payment System (QPS) as a state-of-the-art interbank payments and settlement system designed on real-time gross settlement (RTGS) and electronic straight-through processing (e-STP).
- QCB retail-payment materials surfaced via official site search results describe a centralized domestic payment system for debit and prepaid card transactions across ATMs, POS, and e-commerce terminals, together with a centralized payment gateway for e-commerce transactions with merchants in Qatar.
- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results describe Qatar Mobile Payment (QMP) as QCB’s first instant national interoperable switch for mobile payment services connecting licensed providers in the country.
- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results describe QMP as supporting instant transfers, QR-code merchant payment acceptance, and round-the-clock payment activity.
- QCB materials surfaced via official site search results identify FAWRAN as an instant-payment service and separately describe a centralized payment system launched in 2020 for instant fund transfers between e-wallets within payment service providers in Qatar.
- QCB’s Payment Services Regulation materials surfaced via official site search results document a regulatory framework for payment services within or from Qatar and for QCB-operated switching or settlement systems to ensure payment-service interoperability in the public interest.
- The current source set documents domestic payment coordination, interbank settlement, mobile-payment switching, and instant-payment functionality, but it does not support broader claims about global settlement authority beyond the cited institutional materials.

## Data and Digital Infrastructure

- MCIT’s Digital Agenda 2030 identifies digital infrastructure, digital government, and digital technologies as national strategic pillars and frames data and emerging technologies as part of Qatar’s formal digital-transformation program.
- MCIT states that TASMU Smart Qatar develops critical national digital infrastructure enabling data sharing across priority sectors.
- MCIT states that the TASMU Platform is a cloud-based national digital platform that integrates smart-city services, data sources, and applications across sectors and leverages analytics and AI to turn data into actionable insights.
- MCIT materials surfaced via official site search results identify a Data Management Policy developed to guide government agencies in data management and sharing across the public sector while maintaining data protection and privacy.
- Lusail materials document a city-level data and operations environment in which the Lusail Command & Control Centre combines an operation center with a data center designed to scale smart services and disaster-recovery plans.
- MEEZA states that it operates five Tier III certified data centers in Qatar and describes a geographically diverse, interconnected data-center footprint that supports managed IT, cloud, disaster recovery, business continuity, and a centralized command-and-control environment.
- Microsoft states that it opened a cloud datacenter region in Qatar with Azure and Microsoft 365 services, and Google Cloud states that its Doha region is open with three zones and local data-residency capacity.
- Google Cloud’s Qatar NIA compliance materials document that Google Cloud earned Qatar National Information Assurance certification for its Qatar region, while MCIT materials surfaced via official site search results identify Microsoft’s Qatar region as supporting local cloud services for digital transformation.
- The current source set documents local cloud-region presence, data-center infrastructure, and national digital platforms, but it does not document a fully sovereign hyperscale compute stack independent of global provider participation.

## Internet Exchange and Registry Infrastructure

- CRA states that QIXP is a non-profit, carrier-neutral internet exchange point established for the development of the internet and digital ecosystem in the State of Qatar.
- CRA states that QIXP enables internet service providers, digital content providers, academic institutions, government entities, and private entities to exchange internet traffic within Qatar instead of sending local traffic outside the country.
- CRA states that the QIXP Steering Committee was established by Cabinet Decision No. 24 of 2018, and later CRA materials state that active QIXP participation reached 19 entities as of May 2025.
- The QIX website describes the exchange as an OSI Layer 2 switch fabric in Qatar intended to create local and international bandwidth exchange, simplify interconnection, reduce dependency on international internet connectivity, and attract local and global content providers.
- CRA domain-management materials state that Qatar Domains are administered as a national domain-management surface and link the .qa and قطر. domain space to accredited registrars, registrants, and domain-availability services.
- Official registry search surfaces are published through whois.registry.qa, documenting a live institutional WHOIS lookup interface for Qatar domain-name records.
- The current source set documents domestic exchange and registry governance directly, but it does not support broader claims about global exchange authority beyond the cited institutional materials.

## Telecommunications and Digital Infrastructure

- CRA materials document regulated access to more than 4,860 kilometers of government telecom duct infrastructure across Qatar and identify a CRA-managed Duct Management System used for capacity visibility, application processing, and network-extension coordination.
- CRA materials document an access framework for submarine cable landing stations through consultation and draft standard-offer requirements for licensed owners providing international connectivity services.
- QNBN states that it is the leading national passive fiber service provider in Qatar and that its passive dark-fiber infrastructure provides equal and open wholesale access to telecommunications service providers while supporting the National Broadband Plan.
- Ooredoo states that its fiber broadband and 5G networks form part of its national network surface, describes itself as a pioneer of commercial 5G since 2018, and states that its network covers all municipalities of Qatar.
- Vodafone Qatar states that its 5G network expanded across key locations in the country and described 70 percent of Doha as covered in 2019, framing the rollout as part of building world-class infrastructure in support of Qatar National Vision 2030.
- CRA and QIXP materials document continued localization of digital traffic and onboarding of global and local networks into Qatar’s interconnection environment.
- The current source set therefore documents regulated telecom infrastructure, national fiber access, 5G rollout, and international-connectivity governance through the cited operator and regulatory materials.

## Energy and Industrial Infrastructure

- QatarEnergy states that Qatar is implementing projects to expand LNG production from the North Field, which it describes as the world’s largest single non-associated gas field.
- QatarEnergy states that the North Field East project raises Qatar’s LNG production capacity from 77 MTPA to 110 MTPA, that North Field South would increase it to 126 MTPA, and that North Field West would raise it to 142 MTPA by the end of 2030.
- QatarEnergy’s institutional materials therefore document a state-coordinated LNG expansion program tied directly to named phases of North Field development.
- Kahramaa materials surfaced via official site search results describe Kahramaa as the sole transmission and distribution system owner and operator for Qatar’s electricity and water sector.
- Kahramaa’s published institutional profile therefore documents centralized electricity and water infrastructure coordination within a single national operator.
- Hamad Port materials state that oil-and-gas sector shipments represented through Muntajat are exported via Hamad Port, linking port infrastructure to industrial and energy-export throughput.
- The current source set directly documents LNG expansion and national utility coordination, but it does not justify broader energy-corridor claims beyond the cited institutional infrastructure surfaces.

## Logistics and Global Connectivity Infrastructure

- Mwani Qatar states that Hamad Port is managed under the supervision of the Ministry of Transport, is one of the largest ports in the Middle East, and operates with large container, general-freight, grain, vehicle, offshore-support, and customs-clearance capacity.
- Mwani Qatar also states that Hamad Port supports oil-and-gas exports through shipment handling and related support services and links port operations to rail, sea, and road transshipment.
- Hamad International Airport states that its cargo terminal has capacity to process 1.4 million tonnes of cargo per year and describes the facility as a platform for global freight activity supporting Qatar Airways Cargo and other cargo carriers.
- Hamad International Airport states that the cargo facility includes wide-body freighter stands, airside loading docks, landside truck-loading facilities, specialized temperature-controlled handling, and live-animal logistics infrastructure.
- HIA materials therefore document an aviation-cargo infrastructure layer that combines physical cargo capacity, specialized handling, and direct linkage to Qatar Airways Cargo operations.
- Qatar Airways Cargo materials surfaced via official site search results describe an extensive global cargo network and charter capabilities, which provides additional institutional evidence of aviation-linked global freight integration.
- Together, the cited port, airport, and cargo-operator materials document maritime, aviation, and energy-export logistics surfaces.

## Cybersecurity and National Coordination

- NCSA materials surfaced via official site search results identify the National Cyber Security Agency as the national institution managing Qatar’s cybersecurity compliance and certification structures.
- NCSA materials surfaced via official site search results describe National Information Assurance (NIA) certification as part of the National Information Security Compliance Framework (NISCF).
- NCSA assurance materials surfaced via official site search results document accreditation and audit structures for NIA and related compliance activity, showing that the cybersecurity layer includes formal governance and assessment channels rather than ad hoc guidance alone.
- Google Cloud’s Qatar NIA compliance materials describe NIA certification as a program managed by Qatar’s National Cyber Security Agency and state that certification serves as official recognition of compliance after independent evaluation.
- Google Cloud further states that its certification applies to the Qatar region and references the official NCSA certification-status service, which provides indirect evidence of an operational national assurance surface for digital infrastructure providers.
- The current source set therefore documents national cyber coordination through certification, compliance, and assurance structures, while providing less direct public evidence for operational incident-response workflows than for governance and assurance mechanisms.

## Constraints and Structural Limitations

- QatarEnergy’s published LNG expansion program documents continued national dependence on LNG production and export systems that are inherently tied to global energy markets.
- QCB, QIXP, CRA, and cloud-provider materials place major financial, telecom, and digital systems inside environments that depend on interbank participation, international connectivity, and external provider integration rather than a wholly isolated national stack.
- Google Cloud and Microsoft materials document local cloud-region presence, but the cited infrastructure is operated through global hyperscale providers rather than a documented sovereign hyperscale compute stack.
- MEEZA documents significant domestic data-center infrastructure, but the current source set does not document a fully self-contained national hyperscale platform independent of international cloud ecosystems.
- The current source set does not document a sovereign semiconductor fabrication stack in Qatar.
- HBKU and QCRI materials document research and advanced computing capacity, but the current source set documents a narrower institutional compute base than those of larger jurisdictions with broader publicly documented sovereign-scale compute estates.
- CRA and QIXP materials document local traffic exchange and connectivity localization, but the evidence remains focused on domestic and regional interconnection infrastructure rather than a broader claim of exchange centrality.
- These constraints describe the scope and boundaries of the documented infrastructure surface only and should not be treated as weakness judgments.
