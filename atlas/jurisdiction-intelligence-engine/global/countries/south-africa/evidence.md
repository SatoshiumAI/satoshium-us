# South Africa — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly visible sources only, principally South African government portals, regulators, public operators, public institutional websites, and stable official-site search-visible records where direct extraction was limited by dynamic rendering, redirects, or PDF-heavy publication patterns. It documents observable administrative structures, infrastructure layers, continuity systems, interoperability mechanisms, and operational coordination environments visible in South Africa.

The interpretation here is intentionally operational and infrastructure-oriented. It does not rank South Africa, assign readiness or trust scores, infer hidden capability, infer surveillance capability, infer military or intelligence posture, create geopolitical interpretation, create BRICS interpretation, create economic-power interpretation, or apply gateway, mineral-resource, or strategic-geography narratives. Public evidence is treated as bounded and incomplete by default, and the wording remains renderer-safe by limiting claims to what public sources directly expose.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Publicly visible state infrastructure in South Africa presents as a Pretoria-centered administrative environment with distributed provincial and sectoral continuity layers. Gov.za publicly lists national departments and their ministry relationships, including Home Affairs, Communications and Digital Technologies, Cooperative Governance and Traditional Affairs, Electricity and Energy, Transport, and other sectoral bodies. Separate gov.za provincial-government surfaces publicly expose a distributed provincial administration across Eastern Cape, Free State, Gauteng, KwaZulu-Natal, Limpopo, Mpumalanga, Northern Cape, North West, and Western Cape. This produces a visible national-and-provincial public-service structure rather than a single consolidated operating stack.

Public-service coordination is also visibly layered across specialist institutions. The Department of Home Affairs publicly frames its mandate around civic services, immigration services, the population register, port control, visas and permits, asylum and refugee management, National Targeting Centre functions, and ICT modernisation. The South African Reserve Bank publicly identifies itself as operator, regulator, supervisor and overseer of the national payment system. ICASA, ZADNA, SACAA, SANRAL, TNPA, NDMC, CSIR and other sectoral institutions maintain separate public surfaces tied to regulation, operations, or continuity. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a Pretoria-centered but multi-agency continuity environment in which administrative coordination is concentrated while service delivery and infrastructure operation remain distributed.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

South Africa’s publicly visible identity layer is anchored by the Department of Home Affairs. Gov.za describes DHA as custodian of the identity of South African citizens and publicly associates the department with citizenship, civic status, international migration, refugee protection, and the population register. Official DHA search-visible records further describe the department as custodian, protector and verifier of the identity and status of citizens and other persons resident in South Africa. This is sufficient to identify a formal national identity-administration layer without inferring any non-public monitoring or surveillance capability.

A visible modernization layer exists through Smart ID and related digital-service surfaces. Gov.za states that the Department of Home Affairs began replacing the green bar-coded identity document with the smart ID card and links that rollout to a more reliable National Population Register. Gov.za service pages also state that the department is replacing the green ID book with a smart ID card and publicly expose a process to amend information on the population register. Official-site search-visible DHA materials describe eHomeAffairs as an online service through which eligible users can apply for Smart ID cards and passports, while also stating that branch visits remain necessary for biometric capture or verification. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as an identity-and-service environment with official identity issuance, a population-register layer, online application surfaces, and visible online-to-branch interoperability, while deeper backend identity-validation architecture remains only partly visible.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

South Africa’s publicly visible payment infrastructure is organized around the South African Reserve Bank and the national payment system. SARB states that it has legal responsibility for the national payment system, operates, regulates, supervises and oversees that system, acts as settlement agent for financial institutions, and is responsible for policymaking. SARB also states that the national payment system includes more than 18 payment streams managed by the Payments Association of South Africa under SARB regulation and oversight. This is direct public evidence of a central-bank-administered payment coordination layer.

The settlement layer is also clearly visible. SARB’s domestic settlement materials state that the South African Multiple Option Settlement (SAMOS) system settles domestic high-value transactions, retail transaction batches, and bond and equity market settlement obligations; that settlement participants hold accounts with SARB; and that settlement in SAMOS is final and irrevocable. SARB further states that it operates the real-time gross settlement system for the SADC region and that Vision 2025 defines the public roadmap for safety, efficiency, interoperability, transparency and accessibility in the national payment system. On the retail and market-infrastructure side, official BankservAfrica surfaces identify PayShap as an instant interbank digital payment service available through participating banks, while official Strate and JSE materials identify Strate as South Africa’s principal central securities depository and state that Strate performs electronic settlement for all JSE equity-market trades. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa’s payment and financial infrastructure as a layered environment combining central-bank settlement, interbank retail interoperability, and securities-settlement continuity, without applying financial-center or banking-superiority narratives.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Telecommunications in South Africa are publicly visible as a regulated communications environment. ICASA publicly frames itself as the authority responsible for regulating the telecommunications, broadcasting and postal sectors in the public interest, and official materials describe licensing, spectrum management, numbering, compliance monitoring and consumer protection as part of that mandate. This makes the regulatory layer directly observable even where deeper commercial carrier topology is not public.

A second visible layer is domain-name administration and neutral exchange infrastructure. ZADNA publicly describes itself as the statutory regulator and manager of the .ZA domain namespace and exposes the second-level-domain structure, registry-operator arrangements, policies and nameserver information through its NIC.ZA references. NAPAfrica publicly presents itself as a neutral not-for-profit internet exchange point operating in Cape Town, Durban and Johannesburg, with local peering infrastructure situated in Teraco facilities. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a regulated telecommunications environment with formal country-domain administration and visible internet-exchange infrastructure. Public visibility is materially weaker, however, for private backbone engineering, submarine-cable landing operations, enterprise-network topology, and full commercial operator interconnection maps.

## Electricity & Energy Infrastructure

South Africa’s electricity environment is publicly visible through Eskom and NTCSA. Eskom’s public surfaces expose generation, distribution, tariffs, data, and fault-reporting interfaces, while official-site search-visible materials describe a transmission network of roughly 33,000 km in which supply and demand are balanced in real time at system frequency. Public Eskom materials also show that electricity supplied in South Africa includes Eskom generation together with independent power producer output and imports from neighbouring countries. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a standing generation, transmission-balancing, distribution and import-interaction environment.

A second visible layer is the National Transmission Company South Africa. Official Eskom statements confirm that NTCSA commenced trading on 1 July 2024, and NTCSA’s own public materials describe transmission-network reliability and resilience, transmission-development planning, very-high-voltage line-construction activity, and a public-facing SAWEM School tied to the South African Wholesale Electricity Market. Official Eskom records also state that NTCSA continues the trading arm to and from the Southern African Power Pool. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as an electricity-coordination environment with a visible transmission company, real-time balancing infrastructure, and publicly evidenced regional electricity interaction, while deeper dispatch logic, reserve practice, and private contractual topology remain only partly visible.

## Transportation Infrastructure

Public transport continuity in South Africa is visibly anchored by a national roads layer, a passenger-rail layer and a freight-rail layer. SANRAL publicly identifies itself as the South African National Roads Agency and maintains public-facing mobility, traffic and roads-related service surfaces. PRASA publicly identifies itself as the state-owned enterprise responsible for most passenger rail services in the country, and public materials also expose long-distance passenger service routes through its reservation surfaces. This is sufficient to establish standing road and passenger-rail continuity layers.

A freight layer is also publicly visible through Transnet Freight Rail. Official Transnet materials describe a freight rail service operating on a 22,000 route-kilometre and 30,000 track-kilometre network with linkages between ports, terminals, mines, farms, factories and other production centres. Additional public Transnet materials describe operational integration with ports, port terminals, pipelines and rail-engineering facilities, while rail-infrastructure-management materials expose a separate infrastructure-management surface. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa’s transportation environment as a layered road, passenger-rail and freight-rail system with multimodal overlap, without framing those systems as gateway or corridor assets.

## Aviation Infrastructure

South Africa’s aviation infrastructure is publicly visible through Airports Company South Africa and the South African Civil Aviation Authority. ACSA publicly states that it owns and operates South Africa’s nine principal airports, including O.R. Tambo International Airport, Cape Town International Airport and King Shaka International Airport. Separate airport-facing public pages and official search-visible records make these airport nodes directly observable as continuing elements of the national aviation environment.

The regulatory and safety layer is visible through SACAA. Public SACAA materials describe the authority as a civil-aviation regulator focused on aviation safety and security, while official-site search-visible records describe it as regulating and promoting civil aviation through core oversight functions and maintaining certificate-verification and public reporting surfaces. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa’s aviation environment as a state-regulated airport-and-oversight system centered on major ACSA-operated airports and a standing civil-aviation regulatory authority.

## Maritime & Port Infrastructure

South Africa’s maritime and port environment is publicly visible through the Transnet National Ports Authority. TNPA public materials state that the authority is responsible for the safe, effective and efficient functioning of the national port system, that it manages the port system in a landlord capacity, and that it provides port infrastructure and marine services to South Africa’s eight commercial seaports. This establishes a clear public port-administration layer.

Specific port continuity is also directly visible. Official TNPA materials and official-site search-visible records expose the Port of Durban, Port of Cape Town and Port of Ngqura as active elements of the national port environment, alongside other TNPA-administered ports. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a maritime continuity environment with nationally administered port infrastructure and identifiable coastal operating nodes. Public visibility is stronger for port administration, marine services and port existence than for private terminal operating arrangements, throughput dependencies, or internal commercial-topology detail.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

South Africa’s public emergency-coordination layer is clearly visible through the National Disaster Management Centre. NDMC’s public site exposes an overview and organisational structure, capacity-building and intervention programs, fire services, legislation, strategies, guidelines, seasonal profiles, a geospatial hub, media statements and head-office or centre contact surfaces. Official-site search-visible records state that the NDMC is established in terms of section 8 of the Disaster Management Act, 2002.

The public record also shows functional emergency coordination and early-warning roles. NDMC search-visible materials identify disaster risk assessment and early warning, information-technology and knowledge-management services, disaster-risk reduction and capacity development, and disaster-response and recovery coordination as public functions. The NDMC site also publishes ongoing severe-weather and disaster-related notices. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa’s disaster-response environment as a formal national emergency-coordination system with public information, early-warning and distributed response-support surfaces, while non-public escalation procedures and resource inventories remain outside public visibility.

## Cybersecurity & Data Infrastructure

South Africa’s public cyber-coordination layer is visible through the Department of Communications and Digital Technologies and the Cybersecurity Hub. DCDT publicly states that the Cybersecurity Hub is the national Computer Security Incident Response Team, established by the department, and that it works with government, the private sector, civil society and the public to identify and counter cybersecurity threats. Public materials also expose a national cyber-awareness portal and outreach initiatives such as community-radio programming, newsletters, schools toolkits and hackathon activity. This is sufficient to establish a formal public cyber-incident coordination structure without inferring hidden operational reach.

A broader cyber-and-data infrastructure layer is visible through the CSIR-hosted National Integrated Cyber Infrastructure System. CSIR states that NICIS provides high-performance computing capability, high-speed network capacity and national research-data infrastructure integrated into globally connected systems, and identifies the CHPC, SANReN and DIRISA as its three main pillars. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as having an administrative cyber-coordination layer and a separate national cyber-infrastructure and research-data layer. Deeper defensive tooling, operational incident response methods, intelligence relationships and non-public cyber capability remain outside public observability.

## Research & Education Network Infrastructure

South Africa’s research and education network layer is publicly visible through SANReN, TENET and NICIS. CSIR states that SANReN is one of the three pillars of NICIS and provides high-speed connectivity and advanced networking services. SANReN’s own public service materials expose connectivity services for qualifying research and education institutions, eduroam, performance-engineering support, large-file transfer services and an institutional CSIRT-facing support surface. This is direct evidence of an active national research-and-education networking layer rather than a purely conceptual policy program.

TENET provides the complementary organizational and service layer. Official-site search-visible materials state that TENET was created by South Africa’s public universities as the organisational home for collaborative internetworking by universities, science councils and associated institutions, and that TENET operates the SANReN network for the higher-education and research community. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a research-network-supported environment with institutional academic interconnection, federated service provision and formal research-network operations, while full campus-by-campus topology remains only partly visible.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

South Africa’s regional and international connectivity is publicly visible across payments, electricity, telecommunications, academic networking, aviation and maritime administration. SARB states that it operates the real-time gross settlement system for the SADC region. Eskom and NTCSA public materials show electricity imports from neighbouring countries and a trading role tied to the Southern African Power Pool. NICIS and SANReN materials describe national cyber infrastructure and research-network systems integrated into globally connected systems, while NAPAfrica publicly states that its peering community includes networks from more than 50 countries.

Aviation and maritime continuity also provide observable external interfaces. ACSA’s airport network includes major international airports at O.R. Tambo, Cape Town and King Shaka, while TNPA publicly administers a multi-port maritime environment including Durban, Cape Town and Ngqura. BankservAfrica’s PayShap public surface shows interbank retail interoperability through participating banks, and ZADNA provides a formal country-code domain-governance interface for .ZA. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a regional interconnection environment with multiple external-facing infrastructure layers, without converting those interfaces into geopolitical, leadership or gateway narratives.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of South Africa as both a Pretoria-centered and territorially distributed continuity environment. Administrative concentration is visible through the national-department structure exposed on gov.za and through Pretoria-area and Gauteng-centered institutional surfaces such as DHA, SARB-linked national governance, ICASA, and the NDMC’s Centurion presence. At the same time, critical continuity is not confined to Pretoria or a single metro.

Gauteng continuity is publicly visible through Pretoria-centered administration, Johannesburg-area payment, exchange and air-transport nodes, and NAPAfrica’s Johannesburg exchange presence. Durban continuity is visible through the Port of Durban, King Shaka International Airport, Durban’s NAPAfrica exchange presence, and rail or road interfaces linked to the wider transport system. Cape Town continuity is visible through the Port of Cape Town, Cape Town International Airport, Cape Town’s NAPAfrica exchange presence, and separate provincial-government administration in the Western Cape. These layers overlap with nine-province administrative distribution, national rail and road systems, the electricity grid, maritime administration, and research-network infrastructure. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of South Africa as a distributed territorial continuity environment rather than a single-node administrative system.

## Structural Constraints & Evidence Boundaries

This file is public-source-bound. Telecom visibility is incomplete, especially for commercial backbone routes, private peering arrangements, enterprise connectivity, submarine-cable operations, and detailed operator topology. Private-network visibility is incomplete across banking, telecommunications, enterprise, port, airport and government-contractor environments. Cyber-operational visibility is incomplete beyond the public existence of the Cybersecurity Hub, NICIS-related infrastructure, and published policy or awareness surfaces. Logistics visibility is incomplete for private freight routing, inland distribution networks, warehousing practice, terminal operations and non-public contingency procedures. Commercial-topology visibility is incomplete for bank-to-bank operational dependencies beyond published rails, airport backend systems, private port concessions, and market-infrastructure vendor dependencies.

Several relevant public sites are dynamically rendered, intermittently accessible, redirect-prone, or PDF-heavy, so some narrow points rely on stable official-site search-visible text where direct extraction was inconsistent. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. This evidence layer does not infer undocumented resilience, undisclosed surveillance architecture, non-public cyber capability, or deeper commercial logistics structure beyond what public-source materials directly expose.
