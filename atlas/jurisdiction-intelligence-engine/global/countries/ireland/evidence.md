# Ireland — Evidence Layer

## Overview

- Ireland functions as a sovereign island state in northwestern Europe whose publicly visible continuity depends on coordinated maritime, aviation, road, rail, electricity, gas, telecommunications, and payment systems rather than on extensive land-border infrastructure.
- Publicly documented infrastructure shows the strongest operational concentration in and around Dublin. Dublin Port describes itself as a key strategic access point located at the hub of the national road and rail network and states that it handles 80% of all unitised freight in the Republic of Ireland. daa states that Dublin Airport handled 34.6 million passengers in 2024 including connecting and other passengers, while Iarnród Éireann’s intercity and commuter networks radiate strongly from Dublin.
- Ireland’s infrastructure profile is also shaped by EU interoperability. Central Bank of Ireland materials place Ireland inside SEPA and the Eurosystem TARGET services, while CRU and EirGrid document all-island electricity-market arrangements and interconnection planning linking Ireland to Great Britain and continental Europe.
- Public sources also show a layered digital environment rather than a single network node. ComReg regulates electronic communications and spectrum, the National Broadband Plan extends high-speed broadband into state-intervention areas, INEX operates exchange infrastructure in Dublin and Cork, and OGCIO publishes shared government cloud, network, portal, and data-centre programmes.

## Geographic & Regional Position

- Ireland is the sovereign Republic of Ireland and should be distinguished from Northern Ireland, which is part of the United Kingdom. Cross-border references in this file are limited to directly documented operational interfaces.
- As an island state on the Atlantic edge of Europe, Ireland’s domestic and international continuity is structurally linked to seaports, airports, submarine cables, electricity interconnectors, and digital networks rather than to a large number of overland border corridors.
- Publicly visible infrastructure concentration is strongest in Dublin. Dublin Port, Dublin Airport, Dublin-area commuter rail, Dublin-based government digital platforms, Dublin-centered internet exchange nodes, and major Dublin data-centre campuses all indicate a capital-region concentration in logistics, administrative, and digital operations.
- Ireland’s regional position is dual-facing. It is integrated into EU regulatory, payments, and energy frameworks while also maintaining direct transport, electricity, and gas interfaces with Great Britain and operational interfaces with Northern Ireland where those are formally documented.

## Transportation & Logistics Infrastructure

- Transport Infrastructure Ireland states that Ireland’s primary and secondary road network is about 5,306 km long, including 916 km of motorway. TII also states that this network carries about 45% of the country’s total road traffic and that most of Ireland’s freight is distributed by road. Its control centre monitors and manages more than 65 million vehicle journeys per year.
- Iarnród Éireann states that it provides passenger and freight rail services and operates Rosslare Europort. Its intercity network connects Dublin with Belfast, Sligo, Ballina, Westport, Galway, Limerick, Ennis, Tralee, Cork, Waterford, and Rosslare Europort, while Dublin also anchors DART and wider commuter services.
- Dublin Port states that it is a key strategic access point located at the hub of the national road and rail network. It also states that it handles 80% of all unitised freight in the Republic of Ireland, with most cargo emanating from within 90 km of the port, indicating a strong Dublin-centered logistics concentration.
- Rosslare Europort states that it is a gateway to Europe for freight and tourist traffic, operates three RoRo berths, runs 24 hours a day year-round, and provides direct continental services alongside UK services. This gives Ireland a documented southeastern maritime route that is not wholly dependent on Dublin.
- Other major port functions are regionally distributed. The Port of Cork states that its operations across four locations collectively serve all six shipping modes, while Shannon Foynes Port states that it is Ireland’s largest bulk port company, an EU Core Network Port, and a Tier 1 port in National Ports Policy.
- daa states that Dublin Airport handled 34.6 million passengers in 2024 including connecting and other passengers, with 171 days above 100,000 passengers. The same daa release also records continued Cork Airport growth, indicating a strongly Dublin-centered but multi-airport national aviation structure.

## Energy & Industrial Structure

- EirGrid states that it develops, manages, and operates the electricity grid in Ireland and balances supply and demand continuously. ESB Networks states that the nationwide transmission system carries electricity at 400 kV, 220 kV, and 110 kV from power stations to main towns and cities and connects with electricity systems in Northern Ireland and Britain.
- ESB Networks also documents the physical scale of the transmission layer, including 438 km of 400 kV overhead lines, 1,821 km of 220/275 kV overhead lines, 4,245 km of 110 kV overhead lines, and multiple submarine cables and substations. This indicates a national high-voltage backbone with cross-channel and cross-border interfaces rather than a self-contained island grid.
- EirGrid states that it owns and operates the East West Interconnector and operates the Greenlink Interconnector, both linking Ireland to Great Britain. It also states that it is jointly developing the Celtic Interconnector with RTE as the first direct interconnector between Ireland and continental Europe.
- The Celtic Interconnector project page states that the Ireland-France link will enable exchange of 700 MW over 575 km, including 500 km under the sea. Public materials therefore show continental interconnection as an explicit security-of-supply and market-integration layer rather than only a domestic grid upgrade.
- CRU states that the Single Electricity Market is the wholesale electricity market for the island of Ireland and that it is jointly regulated with Northern Ireland’s Utility Regulator. EirGrid separately states that SEMO is operated jointly by EirGrid and SONI.
- Gas Networks Ireland states that it operates and maintains a 14,758 km national gas network, supplying more than 30% of Ireland’s total energy and almost 50% of the country’s electricity generation, with over 720,000 homes and businesses connected. Gas Networks Ireland also documents Moffat-linked and Northern Ireland-linked pipeline infrastructure through GNI (UK), including subsea pipelines from Scotland and the South North pipeline from Gormanston in County Meath to County Antrim.
- Public energy materials therefore show Ireland’s industrial and continuity structure as depending on coordinated operation of a domestic transmission and gas network together with interconnectors, all-island market arrangements, and grid adaptation for higher renewable penetration.

## Digital & Telecommunications Infrastructure

- ComReg states that it is the statutory body responsible for regulating the electronic communications sector, radio communications, broadcasting transmission, spectrum, and postal services in Ireland. Ireland’s digital infrastructure is therefore publicly organized within both Irish and EU regulatory frameworks.
- Government materials on the National Broadband Plan state that the programme is intended to deliver high-speed broadband to all premises in Ireland through commercial investment plus state intervention in areas where private operators do not plan to invest. The same source identifies a state-intervention area covering more than 560,000 premises, 1.1 million people, more than 65,000 farms, 44,000 non-farm businesses, and 679 schools.
- INEX states that it is a neutral, not-for-profit, industry-owned internet exchange with switching centres in secure data centres around Dublin connected by dedicated resilient fibre links. INEX also states that it launched a standalone Cork exchange in 2016, indicating national internet-exchange activity that is still primarily concentrated in Dublin.
- Aqua Comms documents multiple submarine cable systems connecting Dublin to Great Britain, the Isle of Man, and transatlantic routes to the United States and Northern Europe. Its published network includes AEC-1, AEC-2, AEC-3, CC-1, and CC-2, showing Ireland’s role as both an Irish Sea and transatlantic cable node.
- Equinix states that it operates six Dublin facilities. Together with INEX’s Dublin switching-centre footprint and Aqua Comms’ Dublin cable routes, this provides public evidence of a significant Dublin-area data-centre and interconnection concentration.
- HEAnet states that its national backbone uses more than 2,500 km of fibre, includes a Dublin Core Ring, and provides national coverage across the island of Ireland for education and research connectivity. This adds a separate public-sector research and education network layer alongside commercial telecom infrastructure.
- OGCIO’s Build to Share programme states that government digital infrastructure includes Government Cloud, Government Networks, gov.ie, a Government Data Centre programme, and a National Low Latency Platform. Publicly documented telecommunications structure therefore extends into state-operated shared-service infrastructure as well as regulated commercial networks.

## Financial & Payment Infrastructure

- Central Bank of Ireland materials state that SEPA created a single market for euro-denominated retail payments and that payment service users with an account in one SEPA country can send and receive euro payments across the area under the same basic conditions. Ireland’s retail payment environment is therefore structurally embedded in a wider European payment area rather than operating as a closed domestic system.
- The Central Bank also states that its key responsibilities include the operation of the Irish component of the Eurosystem’s real-time gross settlement system and policy and oversight work for payment and securities settlement systems used in Ireland.
- Central Bank materials on TARGET Services state that these Eurosystem services ensure the free flow of cash, securities, and collateral across Europe. The published service stack includes T2 for payments, T2S for securities settlement, and TIPS for instant payments, all settling in central bank money.
- Central Bank materials on TIPS state that it enables payment service providers to offer fund transfers in real time and around the clock, every day of the year. This places Irish instant euro payments within a Eurosystem-operated settlement environment rather than a standalone national rail.
- Publicly visible Irish payment infrastructure is therefore best understood as a combination of Central Bank oversight, Irish participation in Eurosystem settlement services, and SEPA-based retail interoperability across the EU payment area.

## Government & Administrative Technology Structure

- Department of Social Protection materials state that SAFE Registration is the process used to verify identity and that, once verified, a person receives a Public Services Card. The same source states that the PSC can be used to prove identity when accessing public services in Ireland and to verify a MyGovID account.
- The same official guidance states that verified mobile-phone details can be used to create and verify a MyGovID account for secure online access to public services. Publicly documented administrative access therefore links identity proofing, PSC issuance, and online service authentication.
- OGCIO’s Connecting Government 2030 strategy states that the public service is pursuing a digital-by-default and cloud-first approach, a government-as-a-platform model, stronger data reuse, and application of the once-only principle, while also aligning with EU digital targets.
- OGCIO’s Build to Share materials state that Government Networks delivers carrier-grade, resilient, high-capacity private voice, video, and data services across the public service nationwide, while Government Cloud provides a shared private infrastructure platform for public bodies.
- The same programme also identifies gov.ie as the key portal through which citizens and businesses can interact with government and describes the Government Data Centre as a government-owned facility intended for shared infrastructure use by public bodies.
- Publicly documented government technology structure therefore appears as a shared-services model built around verified identity, common platforms, government-wide network services, portal access, and centrally described cloud and data-centre infrastructure.

## EU, UK & Cross-Border Continuity Infrastructure

- CRU states that the Single Electricity Market is the wholesale electricity market for the island of Ireland and that it was redesigned to harmonise all-island and European wholesale electricity markets. This makes electricity one of the clearest formal cross-border continuity layers involving both Ireland and Northern Ireland.
- EirGrid states that SEMO is operated jointly with SONI, while CRU states that the SEM is jointly regulated by CRU and Northern Ireland’s Utility Regulator. The public record therefore shows cross-border continuity in electricity as a formally governed market and system-operation arrangement rather than as an informal bilateral relationship.
- EirGrid documents electricity interconnection to Great Britain through East West and Greenlink and to continental Europe through the Celtic Interconnector project with France. Ireland’s external energy continuity therefore spans both UK-facing and EU-facing interfaces.
- Gas Networks Ireland documents that GNI (UK) operates and part owns high-pressure pipelines running from Moffat in Scotland to Ireland and the Isle of Man via subsea pipelines supplying the Republic of Ireland, Northern Ireland, and the Isle of Man. It also documents the South North pipeline from County Meath to County Antrim. Gas continuity is therefore explicitly linked to both Great Britain and Northern Ireland.
- Iarnród Éireann states that it jointly operates the Dublin-Belfast Enterprise service with Northern Ireland Railways. This provides a documented rail continuity layer across the border without collapsing Ireland and Northern Ireland into one jurisdictional package.
- In payments and digital administration, Ireland’s continuity is heavily EU-facing. Central Bank materials place Ireland within SEPA and TARGET services, while OGCIO’s digital strategy explicitly references EU digital targets and the once-only principle. These are cross-border continuity mechanisms rooted in interoperability rather than in physical transport links alone.

## Disaster Resilience, Cybersecurity & Operational Coordination

- Met Éireann states that it is the Irish National Meteorological Service and that one of its most important roles is to protect life and ensure citizen safety. It issues weather warnings using yellow, orange, and red categories aligned with international practice and the European Meteoalarm system.
- Met Éireann also states that, where weather emergencies are judged to impact public safety at national level, the National Emergency Coordination Group is activated by the Office of Emergency Planning on request from the lead government department, and that Met Éireann provides weather briefings to the NECG.
- A gov.ie NECG release states that the group brings together all relevant Government Departments and State agencies to coordinate the response to emergencies. The same release describes NECG coordination across transport disruption, fuel distribution, emergency services, ports, agriculture, and health services, indicating whole-of-government continuity coordination rather than sector-isolated emergency handling.
- NCSC materials state that CSIRT-IE provides assistance in responding to cybersecurity incidents at a national level for Ireland, with a constituency mainly consisting of government bodies and critical national infrastructure providers. The same material states that CSIRT-IE monitors incidents, provides alerts and situational awareness, responds to incidents, and participates in the CSIRTs network.
- floodinfo.ie states that the Office of Public Works is the lead organisation for flood risk management in Ireland and that its national flood information portal provides location-specific flood risk and flood management information. The same source states that flood maps and plans support planning and emergency response across 300 communities and 29 river basins.
- Publicly visible resilience coordination in Ireland therefore includes national weather-warning systems, whole-of-government emergency coordination, national cyber-incident response structures, and flood-risk mapping and planning led by OPW.

## Regional & International Connectivity

- Ireland’s regional and international connectivity is strongly maritime. Dublin Port, Rosslare Europort, Port of Cork, and Shannon Foynes together show outward-facing links to Great Britain, continental Europe, Atlantic shipping routes, and bulk-energy or industrial cargo flows.
- Ireland’s aviation connectivity is similarly concentrated but multi-node. daa’s 2024 figures show Dublin Airport as the dominant passenger gateway, while Cork Airport’s official figures and route information indicate a secondary international aviation node.
- Public cable and internet-exchange materials show that Ireland is connected through Irish Sea and transatlantic digital routes as well as domestic exchange infrastructure. Aqua Comms links Dublin to the UK, the Isle of Man, the US east coast, and Northern Europe, while INEX provides domestic peering facilities in Dublin and Cork.
- HEAnet states that it connects Irish learners and researchers to peer research and education networks in Europe, the USA, and the rest of the world. This adds an academic and public-service connectivity layer beyond commercial telecom and cloud interconnection.
- In financial infrastructure, Central Bank of Ireland materials show direct participation in SEPA and Eurosystem TARGET services, embedding Ireland in European retail, high-value, securities, collateral, and instant-payment settlement flows.
- In energy, EirGrid’s interconnector portfolio and Gas Networks Ireland’s UK-linked pipeline infrastructure show that Ireland’s external connectivity depends not only on ports and airports but also on electricity and gas interfaces extending to Great Britain and continental Europe.

## Structural Observations

- Public evidence supports normalizing Ireland as a Dublin-centered operational environment. The clearest concentration appears in port throughput, airport traffic, commuter and intercity rail orientation, internet exchange nodes, data-centre footprint, and multiple state administrative platforms.
- Ireland also functions as a layered island-state continuity system. Maritime freight, aviation, high-voltage electricity interconnection, gas pipelines, submarine cables, and payment-system interoperability all contribute to national continuity.
- Cross-border continuity is real but bounded. The strongest documented interfaces with Northern Ireland and Great Britain are in electricity markets, gas transport, rail service, and some operational emergency and weather coordination context, while EU-facing interoperability is strongest in payments, digital strategy, and future continental electricity interconnection.
- The public source base also shows that Ireland’s logistics, digital, energy, and payment systems are interdependent. Dublin concentration increases efficiency and density in several layers, but it also makes national continuity sensitive to the performance of a relatively small number of major gateways and shared infrastructures.

## Constraints & Structural Limitations

- Bounded visibility limitations apply to this file. It is based on public materials from government departments, regulators, operators, and infrastructure providers rather than on unpublished operational data.
- Infrastructure observability is incomplete. Publicly accessible sources do not provide a full real-time inventory of all ports, roads, rail assets, substations, telecom backbones, cable landings, data-centre capacity, or continuity procedures.
- Regional infrastructure maturity varies across Ireland. Public materials show strong concentration in Dublin and selected regional nodes such as Cork, Rosslare, and Shannon Estuary facilities, but they do not support a claim of uniform infrastructure depth across all regions.
- Public operational visibility is especially limited for cybersecurity, emergency response, government back-office systems, private telecom backbones, and commercial cloud or data-centre operations. Where deeper operational detail was not publicly documented, it has not been inferred.
- Public records also show some live structural constraints, including dependence on maritime and aviation gateways and documented planning constraints around Dublin Airport passenger throughput. These are included only where explicitly stated by public sources.
- Absence of evidence is treated as valid in this file. No hidden capability inference is intended, and no military, intelligence, or otherwise undisclosed infrastructure is inferred from partial public signals.
