# Armenia — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly observable sources only, including Armenian government portals, infrastructure operators, regulators, public-service platforms, and nationally visible network or service providers. It documents only infrastructure, operational coordination, interoperability surfaces, and continuity-relevant systems that are publicly described.

No hidden-capability inference is used here. No rankings, scores, or readiness judgments are applied. No deployment meaning is assigned. No geopolitical or strategic interpretation is introduced. Where public visibility is partial, the record remains bounded to what is directly stated or narrowly supported by public evidence. Phrasing is kept renderer-safe, infrastructure-first, and evidence-only.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Public government materials show Armenia as a Yerevan-centered administrative environment with a nationally scoped executive structure and linked public-service portals. The Government of Armenia structure pages expose decree, session-agenda, and application-tracking surfaces through the official e-gov environment, while the Ministry of Internal Affairs is publicly described as the executive body responsible for internal-security policy and for oversight of agencies including the Police, the Migration and Citizenship Service, and the Rescue Service.

Public-service administration is also visible through ministry and portal linkage. The Ministry of Territorial Administration and Infrastructure public site links the national e-gov portal, a unified portal for online requests, and official e-mail routing through e-citizen.am. This shows that public administration is not only ministry-centered but also linked to shared digital intake and tracking surfaces.

The public record therefore supports normalization of Armenia as a Yerevan-centered administrative system with distributed institutional reach through ministries, agencies, and online service-access layers rather than as a purely paper-based or purely local service environment.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

Armenia’s identity and digital-service infrastructure is publicly visible through EKENG and the official e-gov ecosystem. EKENG identifies itself as the e-Governance infrastructure implementation agency and states that its mission is to facilitate citizens’ communication with the state. Its public materials expose the “Yes em” national system for identification, eID online authentication, mobile identification and signature, digital-signature tools, payment functions, and e-citizen linkage.

EKENG’s about page also states that citizens should be able to view what information is available about them in state databases, use state services via ID card, sign documents electronically with legal equivalence to handwritten versions, and send and track letters without visiting a state body. This makes identity-linked digital access, electronic signature, and state-database interoperability directly visible as public administrative infrastructure rather than as inferred internal architecture.

The official e-gov portal further shows a consolidated state-service layer that includes government decrees, prime minister decrees, session agendas, application tracking, licensing workflows, electronic reporting for licensed persons, electronic state payments, cadastre-related filing, and a unified publication site for legal-act drafts. The migration services platform explicitly states that users are redirected to the “Yes em” national system for identification before returning to the service workflow, which shows identity federation across digital public-service layers.

A separate document-validation layer is also visible. The verify.e-gov.am platform describes itself as a unified system for checking the validity of official documents issued by Armenian state bodies, including civil-status certificates, apostille-certified documents, certain medical certificates, family-status references, and criminal-record certificates.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

Public materials from the Central Bank of Armenia show a centrally coordinated national payment and settlement structure. The Central Bank states that it assumes direct ownership and operational responsibility for pivotal payment and settlement systems within Armenia’s financial landscape and describes those systems as the backbone of national financial infrastructure.

The core interbank layer is publicly identified as the Electronic Payment System of the Central Bank of Armenia. The Bank states that this system has operated since 1997, functions in real-time gross settlement mode, settles interbank fund transfers on a transaction-by-transaction basis, and serves as a platform for both wholesale and retail credit transfers. Publicly identified participants include commercial banks, operators of ancillary systems such as the ArCa card payment system, the Ministry of Finance, and international banks.

The Central Bank also states that it operates the government-securities accounting and settlement system for Armenian government securities and serves as settlement bank for ancillary payment systems including the ArCa card payment system, ArcaPay fast payment system, and Armenia Securities Exchange. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Armenia’s payment environment as a central-bank-centered infrastructure with RTGS, securities-settlement, card-settlement, and ancillary-system coordination layers.

At the same time, public materials are stronger on central-bank-operated rails than on the detailed topology of private financial messaging, bank-to-bank contingency routing, or commercial processor redundancy. Those deeper layers remain only partially visible.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Armenia’s public telecommunications record shows a visible regulatory layer, operator layer, naming layer, and research-network layer, while deeper commercial interconnection topology remains only partly exposed. The Public Services Regulatory Commission is publicly identifiable as the national public-services regulator, and official operator and utility materials show that it confirms tariffs and operates as a formal oversight point across regulated network sectors.

On the operator side, Ucom’s public materials state that it provides fixed and mobile connectivity, as well as television and media services, and that it operates 4G+ and 5G networks. This is sufficient to show the public existence of nationally visible commercial connectivity infrastructure, even though operator marketing pages do not provide a full map of backbone routing, fiber ownership, exchange structure, or redundancy topology.

Naming infrastructure is much more clearly visible. AMNIC states that the registry of the .am and .հայ top-level domains is managed by the Internet Society NGO, and that AMNIC provides uninterrupted operability of the software-hardware network complex in those domain zones while distributing registry information required for domain-name use on the internet. AMNIC’s public FAQ also identifies a registry database and a publicly accessible Whois service for domain-name information.

Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Armenia’s connectivity environment as a regulated and named digital space with visible consumer-service and registry layers, but with incomplete public visibility into exchange, peering, transit, and private backbone structure.

## Transportation & Logistics Infrastructure

Armenia’s transport record is publicly visible as a Yerevan-centered but territorially distributed system composed of road administration, railway services, airport access, and public emergency road-status reporting. Ministry of Territorial Administration and Infrastructure materials publicly present the ministry as part of the infrastructure and transport governance layer, while the ministry site links service-request and e-gov systems rather than operating as a standalone isolated administrative surface.

Rail continuity is visible through the public web estate of South Caucasus Railway. The operator’s English-language site identifies CJSC “South Caucasus Railway,” presents passenger and cargo transportation as core services, and exposes local and international schedule-search surfaces. This is sufficient public evidence to treat railway operations as an active national continuity layer for both passenger and freight movement, even though deeper operational detail such as network condition, line-by-line capacity, and contingency arrangements is not comprehensively exposed through stable public pages.

Road continuity is more visible as an operational condition than as a complete nationally mapped technical system. Public ministry materials and project references show continuing road-infrastructure programming, including official references to the North-South Road Corridor Investment Program. In parallel, the Rescue Service publicly maintains road-condition reporting surfaces, which indicates that territorial road continuity is treated as an active operational-information layer rather than only as static infrastructure.

Taken together, the public record supports normalization of Armenia’s transport environment as a layered logistics and mobility system in which road and rail continuity remain nationally relevant, but where the public map of freight terminals, warehousing, border-processing chains, and multimodal transfer points is incomplete.

## Aviation Infrastructure

Armenia’s public aviation infrastructure is clearly visible through Armenia International Airports and the public airport pages for Zvartnots and Shirak. Zvartnots airport materials identify Yerevan’s Zvartnots International Airport as the main air gateway of the Republic of Armenia and state that airport management was entrusted to “Armenia” International Airports” CJSC under concession arrangements.

The same public materials describe runway renovation, new security, information, and check-in systems, construction of new arrival and departure facilities, and operation of a large new passenger terminal complex. This makes airport continuity visible not only as runway presence but as an operational passenger-processing and airport-services layer.

Public materials for Shirak Airport in Gyumri identify it as the second airport in the republic, document runway overhaul and later renovation, and state that it can be considered the second international alternate airport of Armenia. This supports normalization of Armenian aviation not as a single-node system only, but as one concentrated around Yerevan with at least one publicly visible secondary international continuity node in Gyumri.

Publicly accessible sources do not provide a complete airport-network operations picture, route-risk structure, or real-time contingency map. The evidence layer therefore remains limited to operator, facility, and continuity facts that are directly stated.

## Energy & Industrial Infrastructure

Armenia’s electricity infrastructure is publicly visible through separate transmission and distribution layers. High Voltage Electric Networks states that it operates the 220/110 kV transmission network, provides power-transmission services, maintains and develops the transmission network, carries out construction and reconstruction of high-voltage facilities, and performs other market and system functions defined in its charter and license.

HVEN further states that fifteen 220 kV and 110 kV substations with total capacity of 2561 MVA, one Agarak switching-point/substation site, 330/220/110 kV overhead transmission lines totaling 1914.7 km, and the Lori wind power plant are on its balance sheet. This gives the Armenian evidence base a relatively concrete transmission-layer footprint.

The distribution layer is separately visible through Electric Networks of Armenia. ENA states that it was formed through merger of four former regional power companies, that it is mainly engaged in regulated electricity distribution and sales, that its grid extends about 43,000 km, and that it serves more than one million customers. This makes public separation between high-voltage transmission and end-user distribution directly visible.

Public energy materials also point to broader generation diversity, including wind and other generation assets connected through the transmission and distribution structure, but public visibility is much stronger for grid operators and substations than for a complete, continuously updated national generation map. Accordingly, this layer avoids overstatement about generation mix beyond what operator materials directly expose.

## Gas & Energy Interconnection Infrastructure

Public official materials show Armenia’s gas infrastructure as a national transmission, storage, distribution, and sales environment coordinated through Gazprom Armenia and its Transgaz arm. Official Gazprom Armenia materials publicly describe the company’s main activities as transmission, storage, distribution, and sale of natural gas. Public materials for Transgaz state that the gas transmission system delivers natural gas to urban and rural settlements of the Republic of Armenia and is intended to ensure uninterrupted, reliable, and safe gas supply to consumers.

Cross-border electricity interconnection is publicly visible through HVEN project materials. HVEN identifies a “Caucasian Power Transmission Network I-III” project for Armenia-Georgia transmission line and substation infrastructure and separately identifies the “Construction of Iran-Armenia 400kV Power Transmission Line and Related Substation” project centered on the Noravan substation. HVEN news materials also describe the Armenia-Georgia project as supporting cross-border electricity exchange.

These public sources support normalization of Armenia as an energy-interconnected environment with visible gas-delivery infrastructure and public electricity-interconnection projects toward Georgia and Iran. The file does not extend this into pipeline politics, strategic intent, or hidden regional capability.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

Armenia’s public disaster-response layer is visible through the Ministry of Internal Affairs and the Rescue Service. Government structure materials state that the Rescue Service develops programs for prevention of emergency situations, minimization and elimination of their consequences, protection of the population in emergency situations, implementation of civil-defense activities, creation of civil-defense reserves, fire protection, and fire-rescue services.

The Rescue Service maintains a dedicated public web estate under the Ministry of Internal Affairs. Publicly visible pages and search surfaces show weather updates, road-condition reporting, incident reporting, and emergency-response information. This indicates that emergency coordination is not only an internal ministry function but also a continuously exposed public information layer with operational relevance for national continuity.

The evidence therefore supports normalization of Armenia’s emergency-response environment as a ministry-linked coordination system with civil-defense, rescue, fire-response, and public-alerting components. Public materials do not provide a complete open map of command centers, dispatch topology, or redundancy architecture, so those deeper layers remain bounded.

## Cyber-Coordination & Data Infrastructure

Armenia’s public cyber-coordination layer is visible through the Government Computer Incident Response Center and through identity, naming, and document-validation infrastructure. The Government Computer Incident Response Center states that it collects, stores, and analyzes data on cyber incidents affecting Armenian state bodies, investigates registered incidents, informs state bodies, takes measures to prevent incidents, and cooperates with Armenian law-enforcement bodies during cyber-incident investigations.

The center further states that its main task is to increase the level of cyber security in Armenia, promote the efficiency of cyber-incident investigation, and follow developments in the field while informing relevant bodies when necessary. This is sufficient public evidence to establish a state-body-focused cyber-coordination function.

The broader data-continuity layer is visible through EKENG’s state-database access model, AMNIC’s registry database and Whois surfaces, and verify.e-gov.am’s unified document-validation system. These do not prove a single unified national data architecture, but they do show multiple public data-governance and data-verification surfaces operating within the Armenian administrative ecosystem.

Public visibility remains limited for security-operations topology, defensive tooling, state network segmentation, incident-response capacity by agency, and private-sector cyber coordination. Hidden-capability inference is therefore prohibited here.

## Research & Knowledge-Network Infrastructure

Armenia’s research-network infrastructure is publicly visible through ASNET-AM. ASNET-AM states that it is the Armenian National Research and Education Network and that it is legally represented and operated by the Institute for Informatics and Automation Problems of the National Academy of Sciences of the Republic of Armenia.

Its public materials state that the ASNET-AM backbone interconnects more than 70 organizations in six towns of Armenia at speeds up to 10 Gbps and serves research institutions, universities, libraries, museums, and governmental agencies. ASNET-AM also states that it has had direct gigabit connectivity to the pan-European GÉANT network since 2016, participates in multiple international research-network projects, and operates data-center and e-infrastructure services.

Search-visible federation materials also show Armenian research identity infrastructure, including ASNET-linked eduGAIN-related and federation surfaces. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Armenia as a research-network-supported environment rather than as a purely commercial-connectivity system.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

Public evidence shows Armenia’s regional and international connectivity through multiple operational layers rather than through a single symbolic corridor narrative. In electricity, HVEN project materials make Georgia-linked and Iran-linked interconnection infrastructure directly visible. In gas, Gazprom Armenia materials describe a nationally scoped transmission and distribution system serving urban and rural settlements. In aviation, Zvartnots and Shirak provide internationally oriented airport surfaces. In rail, South Caucasus Railway maintains local and international schedule surfaces and publicly identifies passenger and cargo services.

Digital and knowledge-network interoperability is also visible. ASNET-AM’s direct GÉANT connectivity and federation-related surfaces place Armenia inside a wider research-network environment, while AMNIC’s management of .am and .հայ and its registry/Whois structure expose internationally legible naming infrastructure. The Central Bank’s payment systems and ancillary-system settlement role provide a separate financial interoperability layer within the national economy.

The public record therefore supports normalization of Armenia as a regional interoperability environment with visible electricity, gas, rail, air, payment, naming, and research-network interfaces. It does not support corridor mythology, geopolitical ranking, or symbolic-geography narratives, and none are introduced here.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of Armenia as a Yerevan-centered system with distributed territorial continuity rather than as a capital-only operational environment. Administrative concentration is visible in Yerevan-based ministries, EKENG, the Central Bank, and airport concentration at Zvartnots. At the same time, continuity is visibly distributed through the ENA distribution grid, HVEN transmission substations and lines, Shirak Airport in Gyumri, ASNET-AM connectivity across six towns, gas delivery to urban and rural settlements, railway services, and Rescue Service public road and weather reporting.

This layered continuity matters because administrative services, identity access, payments, electricity transmission, electricity distribution, gas delivery, airport access, railway movement, and emergency coordination do not appear in the public record as isolated systems. They appear as overlapping national layers whose combined function supports territorial continuity outside the capital while preserving Yerevan-centered coordination.

Public visibility is not sufficient to map every province-level dependency, fallback site, or operational reserve. Even so, the available evidence clearly supports a distributed territorial-continuity model rather than a single-node interpretation.

## Structural Constraints & Visibility Limits

Public visibility is incomplete across several important layers. Commercial telecom backbone topology, peering and exchange structure, private data-center geography, rail line condition by segment, logistics-terminal detail, payment contingency routing, and cyber-operational architecture are not fully exposed through stable public sources.

Public sector pages are also uneven in extractability. Some official sites expose clean English-language descriptions, while others are partially dynamic, partially translated, intermittently accessible, or more legible through search snippets than through direct extraction. Where that occurs, this file keeps interpretation narrow and avoids filling gaps with inference.

Absence of evidence here is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. Incomplete visibility remains part of the canonical record. Armenia therefore normalizes, on the public evidence available here, as a bounded-observability environment with visible administrative, payment, digital-service, naming, transport, aviation, energy, gas, research-network, and emergency-coordination layers, but with incomplete public transparency into deeper private or security-sensitive topology.
