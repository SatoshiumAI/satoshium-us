# China — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly visible sources only, principally official Chinese government portals, regulators, public operators, public institutional websites, and stable official-site search-visible records where direct extraction was limited by redirects, dynamic rendering, bot protection, or PDF-heavy publication patterns. It documents observable administrative structures, identity and immigration-service layers, payment and settlement rails, communications and domain-management structures, electricity-grid operators, transportation and aviation systems, maritime and port signals, emergency-coordination environments, cyber and data-governance mechanisms, research-network infrastructure, and distributed territorial continuity environments visible in China.

The interpretation here is intentionally operational and infrastructure-oriented. It does not rank China, assign readiness or trust scores, infer hidden capability, infer surveillance capability, infer military or intelligence posture, create geopolitical interpretation, create sovereignty-position interpretation, create strategic-corridor interpretation, create security interpretation, create technology-superpower interpretation, or convert public infrastructure into civilizational, market-power, or state-capacity narratives beyond what the sources directly expose. Public evidence is treated as bounded and incomplete by default, and wording remains renderer-safe by limiting claims to what public sources directly expose.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Publicly visible state infrastructure in China presents as a Beijing-centered administrative environment with distributed provincial, prefectural, county, township, and sectoral continuity layers. State Council materials publicly describe the country’s administrative units as based on a three-tier system and identify 23 provinces, 5 autonomous regions, 4 municipalities directly under the central government, and 2 special administrative regions. This is direct public evidence of a multi-level territorial administrative environment rather than a single-node capital-only service structure.

Public service continuity is also visibly layered across central ministries and distributed territorial units. Official State Council ministry profiles publicly identify the Ministry of Transport as the executive agency responsible for road, water and air transportation, and identify the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology as a department under the State Council responsible for the administration of industrial branches and the information industry. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a Beijing-centered but territorially distributed administrative environment with standing central ministries and visible multi-level territorial administration.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible identity layer is anchored by resident identity-card law and immigration-service platforms. The Law of the People’s Republic of China on Resident Identity Cards publicly states that resident identity cards register the holder’s name, sex, nationality, date of birth, address of permanent residence, citizen identity number, photograph, term of validity, and issuing authority, and that the citizen identity number is the sole and inalterable permanent identity code of a citizen. This is direct public evidence of a formal nationwide identity-document environment.

A second visible layer exists through immigration and foreign-resident service surfaces. Public NIA materials state that the 12367 immigration service platform includes a helpline, a mobile-phone app, and in-app programs on WeChat and Alipay offering Chinese and English service 24/7, and that the platform’s service has covered 143 countries and regions. Public State Council materials further state that the new version of the Foreign Permanent Resident ID Card supports online handling of travel, daily-consumption, and financial affairs, and that the National Immigration Administration put the new version into use on Dec. 1, 2023. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as an identity-and-service environment with resident identity documents, immigration-service hotlines and digital surfaces, and foreign-permanent-resident credentialing, without inferring non-public identity-verification or monitoring architecture.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible payment infrastructure is organized around the People’s Bank of China. PBC public materials state that China’s payment systems include the high-value real-time payment system, bulk electronic payment system, internet banking payment system, automated clearing house, China foreign exchange payment system, intra-bank payment systems of banking financial institutions, bankcard interbank payment system, city commercial banks’ draft processing system, payment and clearing system of rural credit banks, the Cross-border Interbank Payment System, and the NetsUnion Clearing platform. This is direct public evidence of a layered central-bank-coordinated payment and clearing environment.

A second visible layer exists through continuing public reporting on named rails. Official PBC payment-system reports publicly continue to enumerate the same major rails, including HVPS, BEPS, IBPS, CFXPS, CIPS, and NetsUnion. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a payment-settlement interoperability environment with visible domestic high-value, bulk, internet-banking, foreign-exchange, bankcard, rural-bank, and cross-border payment rails, without converting those rails into financial-center or digital-currency narratives.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Telecommunications in China are publicly visible as a regulated communications and gateway environment. Official State Council materials state that the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology approved China Telecom, China Mobile, and China Unicom to set up international communications gateway exchanges in Nanning, Qingdao, Kunming, and Haikou. This is direct public evidence of an approved carrier and gateway-exchange layer for international communications.

A second visible layer exists through country-code domain and internet-resource administration. CNNIC’s public implementing rules state that .CN and .中国 are China’s ccTLDs, that CNNIC is the ccTLD registry responsible for their operation and management in accordance with the law, and that registrars providing related services within China must obtain the corresponding permission in accordance with law. CNNIC public surfaces also expose .CN and Chinese-domain registration, IP and AS application services, WHOIS lookup, registrar lists, and related internet-fundamental-data materials. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a telecom-domain continuity environment with approved international gateway exchanges, formal carrier participation, country-code domain administration, and visible IP or AS resource-management surfaces, while private backbone topology, carrier-to-carrier commercial interconnection, and enterprise-network design remain only partly visible.

## Electricity & Energy Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible electricity environment is organized through very large state-owned grid operators. Official State Grid search-visible materials publicly state that State Grid Corporation of China takes investment, construction and operation of power grids as its core business and supplies power to over 1.1 billion people. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a large-scale transmission and distribution environment under a centrally visible grid operator.

A second visible layer exists through China Southern Power Grid. CSG public materials state that it is responsible for the investment, construction and operation of power grids in southern China and participates in cross-regional transmission, transformation and interconnection projects. The company publicly identifies electricity supply across Guangdong, Guangxi, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Hainan, as well as Hong Kong and Macao, and publicly exposes an Electric Power Dispatching and Control Center together with branch and subsidiary structures. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as an electricity-grid continuity environment with multiple very large publicly visible grid operators, standing dispatch structures, and cross-regional transmission layers, while fuller balancing practice, reserve structure, market dispatch logic, and non-public contingency arrangements remain only partly visible.

## Transportation Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible transport environment is organized through ministry coordination and very large modal systems. Official State Council materials identify the Ministry of Transport as responsible for road, water and air transportation. Separate State Council policy materials for the 14th Five-Year Plan publicly state that the country is building a modern comprehensive transportation system, that more than 95 percent of cities with populations over 500,000 are expected to be covered by high-speed rail running at 250 km/h and above by 2025, and that improved waterway and aviation networks are also planned. This is direct public evidence of a nationally coordinated multimodal transport environment.

A second visible layer exists through public operating and statistical surfaces. Official transport materials state that 2,776 kilometers of high-speed railways were opened in 2023, 7,000 kilometers of expressways were built or expanded, 1,000 kilometers of shipping lanes were added or improved, the number of airports for transportation stood at 259, and postal services were available to all administrative villages. China State Railway Group publicly states that its responsibilities include unified control and command of railway transport and unified allocation of transport capacity across the railway network. Separate official statistics state that China’s operating railways exceeded 160,000 kilometers in 2024, with high-speed rail reaching 46,000 kilometers and serving 96 percent of cities with populations above 500,000. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a multimodal transportation environment with ministry coordination, large road and waterway systems, dense railway infrastructure, and visible postal continuity.

## Aviation Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible aviation environment is organized through the Civil Aviation Administration of China. Official State Council materials describing CAAC functions publicly state that the administration regulates the civil-aviation air-traffic-control system, divides and authorizes airspace, constructs and regulates airways, supervises and manages communications and navigation, aeronautical information and meteorological data collected for civil aviation, supervises flight safety, and takes charge of safety inspections, airport security, and fire-emergency procedures. This is direct public evidence of a standing aviation-regulatory and air-navigation layer.

Airport continuity is also publicly visible through broader transport statistics. Official State Council and transport materials state that China had 259 airports for transportation in 2023 and added 82 new airports and more than 3,000 air routes over the prior decade. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a distributed aviation environment with a visible regulator, air-navigation and safety functions, and a large multi-airport operating surface rather than a single-airport system.

## Maritime & Port Infrastructure

China’s maritime and port environment is publicly visible through official transport statistics and State Council reporting. Official statistics released through State Council channels state that in the first five months of 2024, port cargo throughput rose to nearly 7.08 billion tonnes and container throughput reached 132.84 million twenty-foot-equivalent units. This is direct public evidence of large-scale ongoing maritime-port activity.

A second visible layer exists through publicly reported major-port interfaces. Official State Council statistical materials state that Ningbo-Zhoushan Port has more than 300 container routes, including more than 250 international routes, connecting more than 600 ports in more than 200 countries and regions. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a distributed maritime continuity environment with very large port throughput and visible outward-facing route connectivity, while terminal-level commercial structures, private logistics dependencies, and detailed inter-port operating arrangements remain only partly visible.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

China’s public emergency-coordination layer is visible through the Ministry of Emergency Management and related warning systems. Official State Council materials state that China established the Ministry of Emergency Management to forestall and defuse serious and major risks and improve disaster relief, and that the ministry is to take the lead in handling major emergency responses and improve the public-safety system. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a formal central emergency-coordination environment.

Operational warning and response layers are also publicly visible. Official State Council materials on natural-disaster response state that China uses a four-tier weather-warning system with red, orange, yellow, and blue levels, and a four-tier emergency-response system with Level I as the most severe. Separate official marine-disaster materials state that emergency responses are classified into four levels, that warnings use the same red-orange-yellow-blue structure, and that coastal natural-resources and marine authorities must formulate local emergency-response plans, with timely transmission and sharing of marine observation data. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a disaster-response and emergency-coordination environment with a visible central ministry, formal warning levels, and distributed sectoral and local response planning.

## Cybersecurity & Data Infrastructure

China’s public cyber and data-governance layer is visible through national law and State Council regulations. The Data Security Law of the People’s Republic of China publicly states that it is enacted to regulate data processing, ensure data security, protect the lawful rights and interests of individuals and organizations, and safeguard the sovereignty, security, and development interests of the state, and that it applies to data-processing activities within the territory of the People’s Republic of China while also creating conditions for certain extra-territorial application. This is direct public evidence of a formal nationwide data-governance framework.

A second visible layer exists through network-data regulations and CAC institutional surfaces. Official State Council materials from Sept. 30, 2024 publicly state that regulations on network data security management will take effect on Jan. 1, 2025, and that they specify general requirements for network-data security, personal-information protection, management of important data, cross-border network-data security, and obligations for internet platform service providers. State Council materials also publicly record the launch of the Cyberspace Administration of China’s official website. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a cyber and data-governance environment with publicly visible legal and regulatory structures for data processing, personal information, and network-platform obligations, while deeper cyber-operational tooling, enforcement methods, and non-public defensive capability remain outside public observability.

## Research & Education Network Infrastructure

China’s publicly visible research-and-education networking layer is anchored by CERNET. CERNET public materials describe it as China’s first and largest national academic Internet backbone, supported by the Chinese government and directly managed by the Ministry of Education. This is direct public evidence of a standing national academic-network environment.

A second visible layer exists through publicly described network structure and operations. CERNET public materials state that it has a three-layer hierarchy of national backbone, regional networks, and campus networks, that more than 450 universities and other education or research entities from all provinces are connected, that the national network center is located at Tsinghua University, that ten regional network centers are distributed among major cities and universities, and that the network has eight international links. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a research-network-supported environment with national academic backbone infrastructure, regional centers, campus connectivity, and visible international academic links.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

China’s regional and international connectivity is publicly visible across payments, telecommunications, electricity, ports, research networking, and immigration-service surfaces. PBC public materials identify CIPS as part of the national payment-system environment. MIIT public materials identify approved international communications gateway exchanges in four cities run by the three major telecom operators. CSG public materials identify power-grid service extending to Hong Kong and Macao and participation in cross-regional interconnection projects. CERNET public materials identify eight international links. These are direct public indicators of outward-facing payments, telecom, power, and academic-network interfaces.

Additional outward-facing continuity is visible through ports and immigration services. Official State Council materials state that Ningbo-Zhoushan Port connects to more than 600 ports in more than 200 countries and regions, while NIA public materials state that the 12367 immigration service platform has covered 143 countries and regions and provides 24/7 Chinese and English service through hotline and app surfaces. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a regional and international connectivity environment through specific payment, telecom, energy, port, academic-network, and immigration-service interfaces, without converting those interfaces into geopolitical, trade-war, or strategic-influence narratives.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of China as both a Beijing-centered and territorially distributed continuity environment. Administrative concentration is visible through State Council ministry structures, national identity-card law, the PBC, MIIT, CAC-facing materials, and national transport and emergency-coordination institutions. This establishes a clear concentration of policy and administrative authority around Beijing-centered state institutions.

At the same time, continuity is not confined to a single administrative node. State Council administrative-division materials publicly expose a nationwide multi-level territorial structure. Railway materials publicly state that the rail network serves 99 percent of cities with populations above 200,000 and that high-speed rail serves 96 percent of cities above 500,000. Transport materials state that postal services are available to all administrative villages and that there are 259 airports for transportation. Electricity continuity is visible through State Grid and China Southern Power Grid, with the latter publicly identifying five provincial-level regions plus Hong Kong and Macao and exposing branch, subsidiary, and dispatch structures. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of China as a distributed territorial continuity environment with overlapping administrative, identity, transport, aviation, electricity, emergency, and communications systems extending well beyond the capital core.

## Structural Constraints & Evidence Boundaries

This file is public-source-bound. Telecom visibility is incomplete, especially for private backbone routes, carrier-to-carrier commercial arrangements, enterprise connectivity, cloud dependencies, and detailed gateway topology. Private-network visibility is incomplete across banking, telecommunications, enterprise, airport, port, and government-contractor environments. Cyber-operational visibility is incomplete beyond the public existence of laws, regulations, institutional websites, and published response or governance materials. Commercial-topology visibility is incomplete for terminal operations, freight-routing dependencies, bank-to-bank backend arrangements, airport-system dependencies, and port-terminal commercial structures. Infrastructure-dependency visibility is incomplete for power-system reserve practice, grid contingency design, private-data-center dependence, and non-public continuity planning.

Several relevant official pages were dynamically rendered, redirect-prone, intermittently blocked, or more accessible through official-site search-visible text than direct extraction at retrieval time, including some State Council ministry profiles, SGCC English materials, and sectoral transport or aviation pages. Bounded observability therefore applies throughout. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. Geopolitical inference is prohibited. This evidence layer does not infer undocumented resilience, undisclosed surveillance architecture, non-public cyber capability, or deeper commercial or interconnection structure beyond what public-source materials directly expose.
