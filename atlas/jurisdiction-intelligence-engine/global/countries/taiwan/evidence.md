# Taiwan — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly visible sources only, principally official Taiwan government portals, regulators, public operators, public institutional websites, and stable official-site search-visible records where direct extraction was limited by dynamic rendering, bot protection, thin page output, or inconsistent page exposure. It documents observable administrative structures, identity and public-service systems, payment and settlement rails, communications and domain-management layers, electricity-grid structures, transportation systems, aviation and port administration, emergency-coordination surfaces, cyber and data-governance mechanisms, research-network signals, and distributed territorial continuity environments visible in Taiwan.

The interpretation here is intentionally operational and infrastructure-oriented. It does not rank Taiwan, assign trust or readiness scores, infer hidden capability, infer surveillance capability, infer military or intelligence posture, create geopolitical interpretation, create sovereignty-position interpretation, create cross-strait interpretation, create security interpretation, create semiconductor interpretation, create supply-chain interpretation, create economic interpretation, create democracy interpretation, create crisis interpretation, or apply technology-superpower or strategic-island narratives. Public evidence is treated as bounded and incomplete by default, and wording remains renderer-safe by limiting claims to what public sources directly expose.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Publicly visible state infrastructure in Taiwan presents as a Taipei-centered administrative environment with distributed municipal, county, district, and township continuity layers. The Executive Yuan publicly identifies itself as the executive branch and exposes official ministries-and-agencies structures. Ministry of the Interior materials publicly state that the ministry handles policies and systems for local government, local organizations, local self-government, administrative divisions, and regional collaboration, while also handling household registration, national identification cards, and household-registration information. This makes both the central administrative layer and a standing territorial-administration layer directly visible.

Official government-portal material also shows that continuity is not confined to a single metropolitan node. Public state information indicates local governments include six special municipalities, thirteen counties, and three autonomous municipalities with county-equivalent status, together with 198 county-administered townships and cities and 170 districts. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a Taipei-centered administrative environment with visible multi-level territorial administration and public-service continuity beyond the capital-facing ministries alone.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible identity layer is anchored by the Ministry of the Interior and the household-registration system. MOI publicly states that it is responsible for household registration and management, national identification cards, nationality-related administration, and household-registration information. Public MOICA materials additionally show that citizen digital certificates are issued by the Certificate Authority of the Ministry of the Interior and that application workflows involve Household Registration Offices. This is direct public evidence of a formal identity-and-registration environment with both national administration and local office access points.

A second visible layer exists through certificate-backed digital-service access. Public MOICA materials state that the Citizen Digital Certificate can be used to access government e-services such as Household Registration Online and File Taxes Online. Ministry of Digital Affairs materials further expose a public digital-service environment that includes the My e-Government Portal, Common Government Application Services, Government Certification Authority, Government Public Key Infrastructure, and Government Service Network. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as an identity-service continuity environment with household registration, national identification, certificate-based authentication, and standing digital-service access surfaces, without inferring hidden verification or surveillance capability.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible payment infrastructure is organized around the Central Bank of the Republic of China (Taiwan). The central bank publicly states that the framework of payment and settlement systems in Taiwan comprises the CBC Interbank Funds Transfer System, FISC Financial Information System, TCH Check Clearing System, and Central Government Securities Settlement System. The same public material states that CIFS acts as a hub of the interbank funds-transfer network and links with the other systems to form the backbone of national payment systems. This is direct public evidence of a central-bank-coordinated settlement environment.

A second visible layer exists through Financial Information Service Co., Ltd. FISC publicly states that its scope of services includes operating interbank information systems, conducting interbank settlement and clearing services, processing financial-information transmission and exchange among financial institutions, and offering disaster backup of interbank information systems. FISC further states that the central bank assigned it to implement a foreign-currency interbank settlement platform compliant with SWIFT standards so that financial institutions can conduct New Taiwan Dollar and foreign-currency remittance through the FISC interbank financial information system. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a payment-settlement interoperability environment with central-bank settlement infrastructure, visible interbank clearing rails, foreign-currency remittance mechanisms, and continuity-oriented backup functions, without converting those layers into fintech or financial-center narratives.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Telecommunications in Taiwan are publicly visible as a regulated communications environment. Public National Communications Commission surfaces expose a commission-level communications authority together with English-language licensing and regulatory sections. Even where direct extraction of detailed commission text was limited by access controls, the official NCC site and licensing surfaces make a standing communications-regulation layer directly observable.

A second visible layer is domain administration and internet-exchange infrastructure. Ministry of Digital Affairs materials publicly state that the ministry manages and supervises the Taiwan Network Information Center. TWNIC publicly describes itself as the organizational center for national network information in Taiwan and identifies functions including domain-name services, IP and ASN registration and management, directory and database access, and coordination with ICANN, APNIC, and equivalent internet organizations in other jurisdictions. TPIX publicly states that it was established in 2002 to provide neutral internet traffic exchange services, supports IPv4 and IPv6, and provides public and private peering services for participating members. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a telecom-domain continuity environment with visible communications regulation, standing .tw and internet-resource administration, and a neutral exchange layer, while private backbone engineering, full carrier interconnection maps, and enterprise-network topology remain only partly visible.

## Electricity & Energy Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible electricity environment is anchored by Taipower. Taipower publicly states that it is a state-owned integrated power utility whose operations include power generation, transmission, distribution, and electricity sales, and that it is responsible for ensuring a stable electricity supply in accordance with the Electricity Act. Taipower further publicly reports a system that includes substations as well as transmission and distribution networks extending across Taiwan. This is direct public evidence of a vertically integrated electricity-service and grid-operation layer.

A second visible layer exists through Taipower’s internal operating structure. Taipower publicly states that it established business divisions for power generation, nuclear power, transmission system, and distribution and service, with a centralized policymaking and decentralized management model. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as an electricity-grid continuity environment with visible generation, transmission, distribution, and service structures under a publicly identifiable utility operator, while fuller market arrangements, balancing practice, reserve structure, and detailed dependency mapping remain only partly visible in the reviewed public record.

## Transportation Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible transport environment is organized through the Ministry of Transportation and Communications and multiple modal operators. MOTC publicly states that its internal divisions include departments for railways, highways and road safety, public transportation and supervision, technology and information for transportation, and navigation and aviation. MOTC also publicly lists external divisions and affiliated bodies including the Freeway Bureau, Highway Bureau, Railway Bureau, Civil Aviation Administration, Taiwan Railway Administration or corporation-facing surfaces, Taoyuan International Airport Corporation, and Taiwan International Ports Corporation. This is direct public evidence of a multi-modal transport coordination layer.

A second visible layer exists through rail and road operators. Official Taiwan Railway Corporation surfaces publicly expose passenger-booking, scheduling, emergency reporting, and customer-service interfaces, evidencing an operating conventional railway layer. Taiwan High Speed Rail Corporation publicly states that it was incorporated to build and operate the high-speed rail service linking Taipei and Kaohsiung over a 345-kilometer route, with twelve stations identified in official operator materials. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a multimodal transportation environment combining ministry coordination, freeway and highway administration, conventional rail, and high-speed rail continuity, without converting these systems into corridor, growth, or strategic-geography narratives.

## Aviation Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible aviation infrastructure is organized through the Civil Aviation Administration under MOTC. CAA public surfaces expose organization sections including air transport, flight standards, air traffic services, airport management, and aerodrome engineering, together with airport and airfield information, Taipei Flight Information Region materials, and air-traffic-services materials. This makes a standing aviation-regulatory and air-navigation layer directly visible.

Airport continuity is also publicly visible through CAA airport materials and MOTC-linked aviation bodies. Official CAA airport and air-traffic materials identify airports including Taiwan Taoyuan International Airport, Taipei Songshan Airport, Kaohsiung International Airport, Taichung International Airport, Hualien Airport, and Tainan Airport, while navigation-aid materials identify additional operational aviation nodes. MOTC also publicly lists Taoyuan International Airport Corporation among its external organizations. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a distributed aviation environment with visible airport, air-traffic, and regulatory layers rather than a single-airport system.

## Maritime & Port Infrastructure

Taiwan’s maritime and port environment is publicly visible through Taiwan International Ports Corporation. TIPC publicly states that it manages seven international commercial ports, namely Keelung, Taipei, Suao, Taichung, Kaohsiung, Anping, and Hualien, and is mandated by the Maritime and Port Bureau to manage the domestic ports of Budai and Penghu. This is direct public evidence of a distributed port-administration layer.

A second visible layer exists through port-group and affiliated business surfaces. TIPC publicly exposes port-group, logistics, storage, towage, and related operating businesses across the managed port estate. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a distributed port continuity environment with multiple officially managed maritime nodes and related port-service structures, without converting those port surfaces into geopolitical maritime, strategic-island, or supply-chain narratives.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

Taiwan’s public emergency-coordination layer is visible through the National Fire Agency under the Ministry of the Interior. MOI publicly states that its subordinate agencies supervise fire prevention and disaster prevention and rescue. The National Fire Agency’s public English surfaces and search-visible official materials expose disaster-prevention and rescue functions, disaster-prevention guidance, and continuing references to the Central Emergency Operations Center. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a formal emergency-coordination environment.

Public NFA materials also expose continuity-support functions such as disaster-prevention tips, incident-related materials, and organization-facing information for coordination across fire and emergency services. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a disaster-response coordination environment with a visible national agency and central emergency-operations surface, while full resource inventories, local escalation procedures, and non-public contingency arrangements remain only partly visible.

## Cybersecurity & Data Infrastructure

Taiwan’s public cyber-governance layer is visible through the Ministry of Digital Affairs, the Administration for Cyber Security, and related coordination bodies. ACS publicly states that it plans national cyber-security policies, reviews, approves, supervises, and evaluates plans, conducts defense, drills, and audits, and supports communications-infrastructure defense and critical-infrastructure cyber defense. This is direct public evidence of a standing cyber-governance and compliance layer.

Public incident-response coordination is also visible through TWCERT/CC. TWCERT/CC publicly states that it promotes incident reporting, coordinates cyber-security intelligence and incident undertakings, interconnects with international intelligence-sharing and analysis platforms, and provides reporting and response, coordination, intelligence sharing, research, and international exchange services. TWCERT/CC further states that operation transferred to the National Institute of Cyber Security in 2024. A public data-governance layer is visible through the Personal Data Protection Act, which states that the competent authority is the Personal Data Protection Commission and establishes rules for collection, processing, use, rights, and coordination concerning personal data. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a cyber and data-governance environment with visible policy, incident-response, and personal-data-governance structures, while deeper defensive tooling, threat-intelligence methods, and non-public cyber-operational capability remain outside public observability.

## Research & Education Network Infrastructure

Taiwan’s publicly visible research-and-education connectivity layer is anchored by TANet. Official TANet surfaces publicly identify the Taiwan Academic Network and expose both a traffic site and a network-operations-center site. Search-visible TANet materials further state that TANet serves as the network and information-education platform for schools at multiple levels. This is sufficient public evidence to identify a standing academic-network environment.

A second visible layer exists through operational monitoring and education-system support. The TANet NOC and traffic surfaces make monitoring and network-operation functions publicly observable even where deeper topology is not fully exposed. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a research-network-supported environment with visible academic-network operations and education-system connectivity, while institutional membership detail, routing topology, and international academic-connectivity design remain only partly visible.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

Taiwan’s regional and international connectivity is publicly visible across payments, internet-resource coordination, internet exchange, aviation, ports, and research networking. FISC publicly states that it operates foreign-currency remittance through a platform compliant with SWIFT standards. TWNIC publicly states that it cooperates with ICANN, APNIC, and equivalent internet organizations in other jurisdictions. TPIX publicly provides a neutral peering environment used by carriers, ISPs, ICPs, and service providers. These are direct public indicators of outward-facing interconnection layers.

Additional cross-boundary continuity is visible through ports, airports, and academic-network administration. TIPC publicly manages a distributed set of international commercial ports and domestic ports. CAA public materials expose multiple airports and air-navigation services, while TANet publicly exposes a national academic-network operations layer. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a regional and international connectivity environment through specific payments, domain, peering, aviation, port, and academic-network systems, without converting those interfaces into geopolitical, alliance, semiconductor, or supply-chain narratives.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of Taiwan as both a Taipei-centered and territorially distributed continuity environment. Taipei concentration is visible through the Executive Yuan, the Ministry of the Interior, the National Communications Commission, MODA, Taipower headquarters functions, and transport and railway administrative surfaces. This establishes a clear administrative concentration around Taipei-centered institutions.

At the same time, continuity is not confined to Taipei. Official public sources expose six special municipalities, thirteen counties, three autonomous municipalities, 198 county-administered townships and cities, and 170 districts. Household-registration and citizen-digital-certificate workflows visibly extend through Household Registration Offices. Transport continuity is distributed through freeway, railway, and high-speed-rail systems; aviation continuity is distributed through Taoyuan, Taipei Songshan, Kaohsiung, Taichung, Hualien, Tainan, and additional airport surfaces; and maritime continuity is distributed through the port system spanning Keelung, Taipei, Suao, Taichung, Kaohsiung, Anping, Hualien, Budai, and Penghu. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Taiwan as a distributed municipal and county continuity environment with overlapping administrative, service, transport, aviation, and maritime systems beyond the Taipei core.

## Structural Constraints & Evidence Boundaries

This file is public-source-bound. Private-network visibility is incomplete across banking, telecommunications, enterprise, government-contractor, airport, and port environments. Cyber-operational visibility is incomplete beyond the public existence of ACS, TWCERT/CC, NICS-linked surfaces, and published policy, coordination, and incident-response functions. Commercial-topology visibility is incomplete for private carrier arrangements, bank-to-bank dependency mapping, freight-routing dependencies, port-terminal commercial structures, and backend service-provider relationships. Infrastructure-dependency visibility is incomplete for power-system contingencies, telecom dependencies, private cloud arrangements, and internal government service dependencies. Operational visibility is incomplete for non-public continuity plans, capacity management, reserve arrangements, and institution-specific failover designs.

Several relevant official pages were dynamically rendered, thinly exposed, or intermittently blocked at retrieval time, including parts of NCC, MOICA, and some transport and local-government surfaces. Bounded observability therefore applies throughout. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. Geopolitical inference is prohibited. This evidence layer does not infer undocumented resilience, undisclosed surveillance architecture, non-public cyber capability, non-public transport contingency design, or deeper commercial or interconnection structure beyond what public sources directly expose.
