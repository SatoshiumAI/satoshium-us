# Costa Rica — Evidence Layer

## Scope & Methodology

This evidence layer is derived from publicly visible sources only, principally Costa Rican government portals, regulators, public institutions, public operators, academic-network institutions, and stable official-site search-visible records where direct extraction was limited by dynamic rendering or access controls. It documents observable administrative structures, infrastructure layers, continuity systems, interoperability mechanisms, and operational coordination environments visible in Costa Rica.

The interpretation here is intentionally operational and infrastructure-oriented. It does not rank Costa Rica, assign readiness or trust scores, infer hidden capability, infer surveillance capability, infer military or intelligence posture, create geopolitical interpretation, or apply environmental, tourism, biodiversity, or sustainability narratives. Public evidence is treated as bounded and incomplete by default, and the wording remains renderer-safe by limiting claims to what public sources directly expose.

## Administrative & Public-Service Infrastructure

Publicly visible state infrastructure in Costa Rica presents as a San José-centered administrative environment with distributed sectoral operators and regulators. Official public surfaces show distinct roles for the Ministerio de Ciencia, Innovación, Tecnología y Telecomunicaciones (MICITT) in digital-government and cybersecurity policy, the Superintendencia de Telecomunicaciones (SUTEL) in telecommunications regulation and FONATEL program administration, the Autoridad Reguladora de los Servicios Públicos (ARESEP) in electricity-service regulation, the Banco Central de Costa Rica (BCCR) in payment-system administration, the Dirección General de Aviación Civil (DGAC) in civil-aviation administration, the Consejo Nacional de Vialidad (CONAVI) in national-road administration, the Instituto Costarricense de Ferrocarriles (INCOFER) in rail services, the Instituto Costarricense de Puertos del Pacífico (INCOP) in Puerto Caldera administration, JAPDEVA in Caribbean port administration, and the Comisión Nacional de Prevención de Riesgos y Atención de Emergencias (CNE) in emergency coordination.

Public-service access is also visibly centralized at the portal level while remaining institutionally distributed underneath. The Portal Pura Vida Digital provides a national digital-service entry surface, MICITT publicly frames digital government as collaborative state modernization across institutions, and RACSA publicly presents service windows that connect users to procedures involving other public entities. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a capital-centered but multi-agency continuity environment in which administrative coordination is concentrated while service delivery is distributed across separate institutional operators.

## Identity & Digital-Service Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s public identity layer is visibly anchored by the Tribunal Supremo de Elecciones (TSE) and its cédula infrastructure. Official TSE service pages and official-site search-visible records identify the TSE as the entity responsible for issuing citizen identification documents and publicly expose cédula procedures, including first issuance, home service in specific cases, and consultation services. This is sufficient to identify a formal national identity-administration layer without inferring any non-public monitoring or data-sharing capability.

A second visible identity layer is the Identidad Digital Costarricense (IDC). Official TSE pages and official announcements describe IDC as a digital version of the physical cédula that enables secure online identification and is acquired through a defined issuance and activation process. For non-citizen administrative identity continuity, the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería publicly maintains DIMEX information and digital-service references tied to migration procedures.

The public digital-service environment is layered rather than singular. The Portal Pura Vida Digital provides a national citizen-service surface, MICITT’s Gobierno Digital section frames institutional digital transformation and certified digital-signature work, RACSA’s Trámite ¡YA! publicly describes a bus de interoperabilidad linking public institutions, RACSA’s Ventanilla Electrónica de Servicios extends passport and DIMEX-related procedures through distributed service points, and the CCSS EDUS application provides an official health-record access surface for users of the Expediente Digital Único en Salud. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as an identity-and-service environment with official identity issuance, public digital-service portals, and visible institutional interoperability, while deeper shared-authentication and backend-integration architecture remains only partly visible.

## Payment & Financial Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s publicly visible payment infrastructure is organized around the Banco Central de Costa Rica and the Sistema Nacional de Pagos Electrónicos (SINPE). Official BCCR materials describe SINPE as a technological platform developed and managed by the central bank that connects financial entities and public institutions through a private telecommunications network for electronic funds transfers and participation in markets organized by the BCCR. Public evidence therefore supports recognition of a centralized payment-rail environment with national institutional reach.

Retail interoperability is publicly visible through SINPE and SINPE Móvil. BCCR materials describe SINPE Móvil as a low-value retail payment service that allows transfers to accounts linked to mobile telephone numbers from electronic-banking channels. This establishes a publicly documented mobile-number-linked retail transfer layer rather than only branch-based or card-based payment continuity.

A securities-settlement layer is also publicly visible. The BCCR’s Liquidación de Mercados (LIM) service states that it settles Costa Rican securities-market operations, that the Bolsa Nacional de Valores sends multilateral net cash and securities positions for settlement in BCCR-held accounts, and that liquidation is carried out through SINPE’s interbank settlement services under delivery-versus-payment logic. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica’s payment and financial infrastructure as a layered environment combining central-bank-administered payment rails, retail transfer interoperability, and securities-settlement continuity, without invoking financial-center or banking-superiority narratives.

## Telecommunications & Connectivity Infrastructure

Telecommunications in Costa Rica are publicly visible as a regulated public-service environment. SUTEL publicly presents itself as the telecommunications regulator and exposes market reports, spectrum information, FONATEL program materials, and a telecommunications registry. Official operator-registry surfaces and authorized-operator listings make the regulatory and registration layer directly observable even where full commercial backbone topology is not public.

A second visible layer is publicly supported connectivity expansion. SUTEL’s Espacios Públicos Conectados program states that FONATEL financed a national high-capacity network with more than 2,500 kilometers of fiber optic infrastructure to provide free Wi-Fi access in 513 public spaces, including parks, plazas, train stations, libraries, and civic centers. This establishes a documented public-access connectivity layer that overlaps with broader territorial continuity rather than a purely commercial retail network.

A third visible layer is national domain administration. NIC Costa Rica publicly presents the .cr domain-registration environment and domain-registration rules. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a regulated telecommunications environment with visible operator registration, public-connectivity programs, and formal country-domain administration. Public visibility is materially weaker, however, for private carrier interconnection, exchange structure, submarine-cable topology, and non-public enterprise-network architecture.

## Electricity & Energy Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s electricity environment is publicly visible through an operating-control layer, a distribution layer, and a regulatory layer. ICE publicly exposes an operation-and-control surface through its División Operación y Control del Sistema Eléctrico, while public ICE electricity-service surfaces expose billing, outage consultation, and service interfaces. This makes national electricity administration and at least part of the operational-control surface publicly visible, even though full technical grid topology is not.

Distribution continuity is also visible through CNFL, which publicly maintains electricity-service information, outage-reporting references, and documentation covering service topics including smart electrical networks and technical requirements. ARESEP publicly states that it regulates electricity service across generation, transmission, distribution, and public lighting. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as an electricity-coordination environment with visible operational control, distribution-service administration, and sector regulation.

Regional electrical interconnection is also publicly observable through the regional operator environment. The Ente Operador Regional publicly maintains regional-market, operational-planning, and Costa Rica-specific commercial-reporting surfaces. Public evidence therefore supports recognition of Costa Rica as part of a regional interconnection environment, while detailed contingency procedures, reserve arrangements, and deeper dispatch engineering remain only partly visible in public sources.

## Transportation Infrastructure

Public transport continuity in Costa Rica is visibly anchored by road administration and a narrower rail layer. CONAVI publicly identifies itself as the entity responsible for conservation and construction of roads, travesía segments, and bridges within the national road network, and it publicly maintains national road-network and map surfaces. This is sufficient public evidence to identify road continuity as a standing national administrative function.

A rail layer is also publicly visible through INCOFER. Its public passenger-transport surface establishes continuing rail-service administration and makes passenger rail part of the observable national transportation environment. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica’s transportation environment as a layered road-and-rail system whose continuity overlaps with airports, ports, and public-connectivity infrastructure.

The public record is more detailed for institutional administration than for freight topology or private logistics networks. Accordingly, this section remains focused on road continuity, public rail administration, and multimodal overlap rather than broader corridor or gateway interpretation.

## Aviation Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s aviation infrastructure is publicly visible through a national civil-aviation administrative layer and separate airport-operator surfaces. DGAC publicly maintains an aviation-administration surface and an international-airports surface, and official search-visible DGAC materials identify Juan Santamaría, Daniel Oduber Quirós, Tobías Bolaños, and Limón within the country’s international-airport environment. This is sufficient to establish a formal national aviation administration layer.

Juan Santamaría International Airport also maintains a public operator-facing surface that exposes airport information, contacts, airline listings, and passenger services. Official Guanacaste Airport materials for Daniel Oduber Quirós International Airport publicly describe the airport as an active international airport with a continuing terminal and route operation surface. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica’s aviation environment as a state-administered and operator-exposed continuity system centered on Juan Santamaría and Daniel Oduber Quirós, without applying tourism framing.

## Maritime & Port Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s maritime and port environment is publicly visible through separate Pacific and Caribbean administrative layers. INCOP’s Puerto Caldera materials document the Pacific-side port’s historical development, state administration, and concession-era continuity. This is sufficient to identify a standing Pacific port-administration layer tied to public institutional oversight.

On the Caribbean side, JAPDEVA publicly documents the Complejo Portuario Limón-Moín as a two-terminal environment composed of the Hernán Garrón Salazar terminal in Limón and the Gastón Kogan Kogan terminal in Moín, with publicly listed berths and operational references. This makes a multi-terminal Caribbean port layer directly observable from official sources.

Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a maritime continuity environment with distinct Pacific and Caribbean port-administration structures. Public visibility is stronger for port existence, administration, and basic operating configuration than for private operator arrangements, commercial throughput relationships, or deeper terminal-topology detail.

## Disaster-Response & Emergency Coordination Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s public emergency-coordination layer is clearly visible through the CNE. Official CNE materials publicly define national alert states, maintain current and historical alert publications, and publish hazard-map resources. This is direct evidence of a standing public-alert and emergency-coordination surface rather than an ad hoc response environment.

The public record also shows a multi-hazard framing. CNE materials explicitly describe Costa Rica as exposed to multiple natural and anthropogenic threats and maintain operational alert references tied to developing events. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica’s disaster-response environment as a formal national emergency-coordination system with public alerting and distributed territorial applicability. The public record is less complete for internal escalation procedures, resource inventories, and non-public continuity plans.

## Cybersecurity & Data Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s public cyber-coordination layer is visible through MICITT and CSIRT-CR. Official MICITT cybersecurity pages and official-site search-visible records identify CSIRT-CR as the national computer-security incident-response center, created by decree and based within MICITT, with coordination functions that extend across the powers of the state, autonomous institutions, companies, and state banks. This is sufficient to establish a formal public cyber-incident response structure without inferring hidden operational reach.

Public digital-governance infrastructure is also visible in adjacent MICITT systems. MICITT’s Gobierno Digital materials frame digital government as collaborative institutional modernization and explicitly group cybersecurity, digital governance, artificial intelligence, and certified digital signature within one public policy surface. The national cybersecurity strategy is also publicly available through MICITT. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as having an administrative cyber-coordination layer and visible government digital-governance infrastructure, while deeper operational tooling, classified response capability, and private-sector security architecture remain outside public visibility.

## Research & Education Network Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s research and education network layer is publicly visible through RedCONARE and CONARE. Official CONARE materials describe RedCONARE as Costa Rica’s national research and education network, tied to the Consejo Nacional de Rectores and connected to RedCLARA since 2008. The same public record describes RedCONARE as integrating the state universities together with the CONARE site and CeNAT.

Public materials also identify concrete connectivity services. CONARE states that RedCONARE supports services including eduroam and describes a 5 Gbps connection environment. These materials also show overlap between academic-network connectivity and wider public-access infrastructure through SUTEL and FONATEL public-space Wi-Fi programs. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a research-network-supported environment with institutional academic interconnection and international research-network participation, without extending that evidence into innovation-state or knowledge-economy rhetoric.

## Regional & International Connectivity Infrastructure

Costa Rica’s regional and international connectivity is publicly visible across electricity, aviation, maritime administration, academic networking, internet naming, and payment interoperability. In electricity, the Ente Operador Regional maintains public regional-market and technical-operational surfaces that include Costa Rica-specific reporting. In academic networking, RedCONARE’s public linkage to RedCLARA establishes a formal international research-network interface.

In aviation, DGAC’s international-airport administration and the public operator surfaces of Juan Santamaría and Daniel Oduber Quirós expose continuing external passenger and airline interfaces. In maritime administration, the coexistence of Puerto Caldera and the Limón-Moín complex provides observable Pacific and Caribbean maritime continuity surfaces. In telecommunications and internet governance, NIC Costa Rica provides a formal administrative interface for the .cr country-code domain. In payments, BCCR documents interoperability across financial entities and public institutions through SINPE and SINPE Móvil. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a regional interconnection environment with multiple external interfaces, without treating any one of them as a leadership or geopolitical narrative.

## Distributed Territorial Continuity Infrastructure

Public evidence supports normalization of Costa Rica as both a San José-centered and territorially distributed continuity environment. Administrative concentration is visible through the public institutional presence of TSE, MICITT, BCCR, SUTEL, CNE, CONAVI, and other national bodies in the capital-centered state apparatus. At the same time, continuity is not confined to San José. Public infrastructure records show Juan Santamaría operations in Alajuela, Daniel Oduber Quirós operations in Guanacaste, Puerto Caldera on the Pacific coast, the Limón-Moín port complex on the Caribbean coast, distributed train stations tied to public-connectivity programs, and public-service interfaces that extend beyond central offices.

This creates overlapping continuity layers rather than a purely centralized system. Identity services, migration-document services, digital-government portals, payment rails, telecommunications regulation, public Wi-Fi infrastructure, electricity-service interfaces, road administration, rail services, airports, ports, emergency alerts, and academic-network connectivity all contribute to continuity across more than one territory and infrastructure class. Public evidence therefore supports normalization of Costa Rica as a layered transport, payment, digital, energy, maritime, and public-service environment whose continuity extends beyond the capital even though administrative coordination remains strongly concentrated there.

## Structural Constraints & Evidence Boundaries

This file is public-source-bound. Telecom visibility is incomplete, especially for commercial backbone routes, carrier interconnection, private fiber engineering, exchange structure, and private enterprise-network architecture. Private-network visibility is incomplete across banking, telecom, enterprise, and port-operator environments. Cyber-operational visibility is incomplete beyond the public existence of CSIRT-CR, MICITT policy surfaces, and published strategy or coordination references. Logistics visibility is incomplete for private freight routing, inland warehousing, terminal operating practice, and commercial contracting structure. Commercial-topology visibility is incomplete for bank settlement arrangements beyond published BCCR rails, airport operator backend systems, port concession detail, and deeper service-provider dependencies.

Several relevant public sites are dynamically rendered, intermittently accessible, or protected by anti-bot controls, so some narrow points rely on stable official-site search-visible text where direct extraction was inconsistent. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Hidden-capability inference is prohibited. This evidence layer does not infer undocumented resilience, undisclosed cyber capability, non-public surveillance architecture, or deeper commercial logistics structure beyond what public-source materials directly expose.
